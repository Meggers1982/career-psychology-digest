### 1. Why the same perk keeps a nonprofit worker but not a corporate one

**Journal:** *Personnel Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-10
**Work ID:** W7212127227 | **DOI:** 10.1111/peps.70042

**The study:** Researchers ran four linked studies comparing how employee-centered management practices — benefits, participation in decisions, and training — play out in nonprofits versus for-profit firms. A preliminary study compared employee ratings across sectors; Study 1 used archival Statistics Canada workplace survey data and found these practices were linked to lower voluntary turnover in nonprofits than in for-profits; a qualitative study pointed to psychological contract fulfillment as the mechanism; and scenario experiments tested that mediation directly. The same HR investment appeared to buy more loyalty in the nonprofit setting, apparently because it confirms employees' sense that the organization keeps its implicit promises.

**Why it matters:** Nonprofits competing for talent against better-paying employers may get disproportionate retention returns from participation, training and benefits — and for-profit managers learn that identical practices don't produce identical loyalty when employees read them against different expectations.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For managers: before benchmarking HR spend against other sectors, ask what employees believe your organization promised them. The archival turnover evidence suggests practices that signal 'we meant what we said' outperform generic perks — though the mechanism was tested with scenarios, not live policy changes.
- **For general readers:** For readers weighing a mission-driven job against a corporate one: the research suggests the value of training, voice and benefits depends partly on whether they match what you thought you signed up for, which is one reason people leave jobs that look good on paper.

**Caveats:** Mixed evidence quality: the turnover result is archival and correlational, the mediation test rests on hypothetical scenario experiments rather than real decisions, and the archival data are Canadian only.

### 2. Meta-analysis: workplace chameleons impress bosses more than they actually perform

**Journal:** *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-08-10
**Work ID:** W7202110265 | **DOI:** 10.1111/joop.70139

**The study:** A meta-analysis of 35 samples examined the long-assumed link between self-monitoring — the tendency to read a room and adjust your behavior accordingly — and job performance. The overall correlation was modest (r = .12), and moderator analyses showed it appeared only in supervisor ratings, not in objective outcome measures, and only for contextual performance (helping, cooperating), not core task performance. The authors conclude self-monitoring is more about style, in the sense of shaping raters' perceptions, than substance.

**Why it matters:** If performance ratings reward social adaptability rather than output, organizations may be promoting impression managers and misreading who actually delivers — a direct challenge to the common belief that political skill equals effectiveness.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For managers: audit whether your appraisal system can distinguish contribution from polish. Pair supervisor ratings with outcome data on at least some roles, and notice if your highest-rated people cluster among the most socially attuned rather than the most productive.
- **For general readers:** For readers: adapting your persona to the room really does seem to pay in how you're seen at work — just not, on this evidence, in what you actually produce. That gap explains a lot of office frustration about who gets ahead.

**Caveats:** Small overall effect; moderator effects are themselves described as modest with substantial residual heterogeneity; meta-analytic correlations cannot establish causation, and the number of samples (35) is modest for strong moderator claims.

### 3. When men's scores vary more, the selection test picks a workforce that's both mostly male and weaker

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-03
**Work ID:** W7207756322 | **DOI:** 10.1037/apl0001409

**The study:** Published in the *Journal of Applied Psychology*, this work models the consequences of 'greater male variability' — the tendency for men's scores on many ability and personality measures to be more spread out than women's, even at similar average levels — for the output of selection systems. The analysis shows that when such measures are used to select the top scorers, women end up underrepresented, and that selection systems with certain common design attributes yield a workforce that is not only predominantly male but also lower in expected job performance than alternatives. The authors propose design changes that reduce the distorting influence of variability differences on hiring decisions.

**Why it matters:** It reframes a core diversity problem as a measurement and system-design problem: the same top-down selection rule that produces skewed gender composition can also leave performance on the table, meaning fairness and quality fixes can point in the same direction.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For managers and HR leaders: examine how your cut scores, top-down ranking and banding rules interact with the distribution — not just the average — of candidate scores. The paper argues specific, fixable design attributes drive the skew, so this is a question for your assessment vendor, not just your diversity statement.
- **For general readers:** For readers: this is a concrete answer to why 'we just hire the top scorers' doesn't automatically mean merit — the shape of the score distribution, not only who is better on average, shapes who ends up in the room.

**Caveats:** The abstract supplied is truncated; the work appears to be analytical/simulation-based modeling of selection systems rather than a study of hired employees, so conclusions depend on the assumed distributions and selection parameters.

### 4. Engagement is a daily state — and assertiveness on the day predicts it

**Journal:** *Journal of Business and Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-28
**Work ID:** W7214632122 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10869-026-10147-7

**The study:** Ninety-five full-time employees completed daily diaries across 10 workdays, producing 804 daily observations linking day-level personality states to day-level work engagement. Narrow facets did extra explanatory work: state assertiveness predicted that day's engagement over and above lagged engagement, overall state extraversion and positive mood, while state anxiety predicted engagement beyond state neuroticism and negative mood. In other words, how assertive or anxious someone felt that day mattered independently of their general disposition or mood.

**Why it matters:** It supports treating engagement as something that fluctuates day to day and is tied to specific, potentially malleable states rather than a fixed personality trait that managers can only hire for.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For managers: if engagement moves daily, engagement surveys once a year tell you little. Consider whether your meeting design, workload and psychological safety make it easy or hard for people to speak up on a given day — assertiveness on the day was the standout predictor.
- **For general readers:** For readers: a flat, disengaged Tuesday isn't necessarily evidence you're in the wrong job. The data show engagement swings within the same person across a short period, tracking how assertive or anxious you feel that day.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=95), all self-report from a single source so common-method bias is plausible, correlational within-person design over only 10 workdays, and occupation/country details are limited.

### 5. Followers go the extra mile for a boss who seems overqualified — unless they're competitive narcissists

**Journal:** *Journal of Organizational Behavior* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214754642 | **DOI:** 10.1002/job.70132

**The study:** Across one US vignette experiment and two multi-wave, multi-source surveys of employees in China, researchers tested how perceiving your leader as overqualified for the role affects your behavior toward them. Seeing a leader as overqualified strengthened followers' personal identification with that leader, which in turn predicted more organizational citizenship behavior directed at the leader. The effect depended on follower personality: narcissistic admiration amplified the identification pathway, while narcissistic rivalry suppressed it.

**Why it matters:** Overqualification is usually studied as a liability for the overqualified employee; here it's an asset that spills over, with a leader's surplus credentials making followers more willing to help them — but not with every follower.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For managers: a leader who is 'too good' for the job may be a team-level advantage, not just a flight risk. Watch for the subset of direct reports who respond to a high-status boss with rivalry rather than admiration, since the same leader attribute moves them in the opposite direction.
- **For general readers:** For readers: how you react to a boss with more credentials than the job requires — inspired or threatened — says as much about you as about them, and shapes how much discretionary help you end up giving.

**Caveats:** One study uses hypothetical vignettes; the field evidence comes from two surveys in China, limiting generalization; citizenship behavior toward the leader is measured relationally and outcomes rest partly on self-reported perceptions of the leader.

### 6. What makes over-50s want to keep working — and why it doesn't apply to managers

**Journal:** *Personnel Review* | **Published:** 2026-09-28
**Work ID:** W7214630663 | **DOI:** 10.1108/pr-06-2025-0665

**The study:** A survey of 432 employees aged 50 and over in a Dutch local government examined whether job resources predict willingness to continue working in their current role. Job satisfaction fully mediated the effects of autonomy and social support, and partially mediated the effect of learning opportunities. Crucially, job category moderated the satisfaction–willingness link: it was strong for professionals and support staff but absent among those in managerial positions.

**Why it matters:** With aging workforces and pension ages rising, employers trying to retain older staff may need different levers for different job families — job satisfaction appears to be a poor retention lever for older managers.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For HR and managers: if you're redesigning roles to keep experienced staff longer, autonomy, social support and learning opportunities are the resources that translate into willingness to stay — but don't assume the same satisfaction-based logic works for your older managers, for whom the link didn't appear.
- **For general readers:** For readers approaching their 50s and 60s: the decision to keep working often turns less on pay than on whether the job still offers control, colleagues and something new to learn.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional self-report survey — no causal inference; single organization (one Dutch local government) and single country; the managerial subgroup is likely small, so the null finding there should be treated as tentative.

### 7. The IQ test's grip on hiring just got weaker: new meta-analysis puts the validity at .20, not .50

**Journal:** *Journal of Business and Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-08-25
**Work ID:** W7203815879 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10869-026-10146-8

**The study:** Researchers obtained the raw datasets behind three recent competing meta-analyses of general mental ability (GMA) and job performance, covering studies published between 1990 and 2022, and pooled them into a single comprehensive analysis (total k=212 samples, N=109,396 workers). The mean operational validity of cognitive ability for overall job performance came out at .18 to .22 — far below the near-.50 figure that dominated textbooks and was based largely on pre-1980 data. Moderator analyses using O*NET job complexity dimensions found little link between cognitive complexity ("Data") and validity, but a positive relationship for manual complexity ("Things"), contradicting the long-standing assumption that intelligence tests matter most in cognitively demanding jobs.

**Why it matters:** Cognitive ability testing has been defended for decades as the most powerful and defensible selection tool available, and that claim has shaped hiring budgets, vendor pitches and legal arguments; the contemporary estimate suggests it explains roughly 4% of performance variance, not 25%.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If your organization buys cognitive ability tests, ask the vendor which meta-analysis their validity claims rest on and whether the data predate 1980 — and reconsider how much weight a test score should carry relative to structured interviews, work samples and job knowledge assessments.
- **For general readers:** For job seekers who have felt reduced to an aptitude score, this is evidence that the predictive power of such tests over actual job performance is modest — and that the "smart people do better at complex jobs" assumption is shakier than it sounds.

**Caveats:** Meta-analytic operational validities are corrected estimates, not observed correlations, and the debate is precisely about how much correction is appropriate; the analysis reuses overlapping datasets assembled by other teams, and validity coefficients reflect correlation with supervisor-rated performance rather than proven causation.

### 8. Your most motivated employee may be the one dodging colleagues in the hallway

**Journal:** *Organization Science* | **Published:** 2026-08-21
**Work ID:** W7203894267 | **DOI:** 10.1287/orsc.2024.18802

**The study:** Across three studies — an online experiment with a U.S. sample, a field experiment in China, and a survey of Chinese employees — researchers tested whether high task motivation can produce a maladaptive side effect: avoiding interaction with coworkers. When employees perceived their work as low in task interdependence, those with higher task motivation were more likely to dodge social interaction in order to protect their focus, and that avoidance was in turn associated with lower job performance.

**Why it matters:** Managers typically treat motivation as an unmitigated good, but the findings suggest highly driven employees in seemingly independent roles can cut themselves off from the information and coordination they actually need to perform.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you manage someone who is head-down and highly driven, check whether they understand how interdependent their work really is — making the hand-offs and downstream dependencies explicit may be what converts their motivation into results rather than isolation.
- **For general readers:** The familiar instinct to close the door, decline the meeting and "just get it done" can quietly cost you — especially when you underestimate how much your output depends on other people's.

**Caveats:** Two of three samples are Chinese and the online experiment uses a panel-type U.S. sample; the survey component is self-report and cannot establish causation; funded by the National Natural Science Foundation of China.

### 9. Playing favorites doesn't automatically backfire — if the favored employee is you, and you know it

**Journal:** *Journal of Management* | **Published:** 2026-08-20
**Work ID:** W7203857104 | **DOI:** 10.1177/01492063261467674

**The study:** Three studies — a preregistered experiment, a single-level multi-wave survey, and a multilevel, multi-wave, multisource study — examined how followers react when a leader visibly treats team members differently. Differentiation generally lowered followers' sense of being treated fairly by their supervisor, which then increased supervisor-directed deviance and reduced citizenship behavior toward the boss. But when a follower's own relationship quality with the leader was high and they compared favorably to teammates, the damaging effect of differentiation shrank to statistically zero.

**Why it matters:** Leaders inevitably build closer relationships with some reports than others; the research pinpoints that the harm comes from followers' justice perceptions, and that it falls disproportionately on those who sense they are on the losing end of the comparison.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Before assuming your team can handle visible differences in how you treat people, audit who is likely to perceive themselves as the less-favored party — those are the relationships where unfairness perceptions, withdrawn goodwill and subtle pushback will show up first.
- **For general readers:** Much of what feels like office politics is really social comparison: how you feel about your boss depends less on how they treat you than on how they treat you relative to the person at the next desk.

**Caveats:** One component is a scenario-based experiment; the field components rely substantially on follower self-report for justice perceptions and deviance, and the designs are correlational over time rather than causal outside the experiment.

### 10. A short video and a writing exercise cut employee exhaustion two weeks later

**Journal:** *European Journal of Work and Organizational Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-30
**Work ID:** W7215081402 | **DOI:** 10.1080/1359432x.2026.2736701

**The study:** Researchers randomly assigned 120 employees to an online "stress mindset" micro-intervention — a video about the potential benefits of stressful situations plus a writing task — or to an alternative-treatment or passive control condition, with half the intervention group getting a booster session a week later. Two weeks on, the intervention had shifted participants' stress mindset with no fade-out, and both intervention groups reported lower exhaustion and were less likely to appraise work demands as hindrances. Unexpectedly, the no-booster group also showed a decline in challenge appraisal.

**Why it matters:** It suggests a very cheap, low-burden digital exercise can move how employees interpret job stress and reduce exhaustion — a contrast to expensive multi-session wellness programs.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Worth testing as a low-cost supplement to wellness offerings, but treat it as a complement to fixing workload, not a substitute: reframing stress is not the same as reducing it, and the unexpected drop in challenge appraisal is a warning sign that deserves tracking.
- **For general readers:** How you interpret a stressful week — as a threat to survive or a demand you can meet — appears to be partly trainable, and the shift held for at least two weeks.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=120 split across three conditions), short two-week horizon, self-report outcomes, and a counterintuitive negative finding on challenge appraisal; the booster did not clearly improve results.

### 11. When nursing home workers were happier at work, their blood showed less inflammation

**Journal:** *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-08-10
**Work ID:** W7202070440 | **DOI:** 10.1037/ocp0000447

**The study:** Researchers followed 799 nursing home employees across four measurement points over 18 months, combining surveys with blood-based C-reactive protein, a marker of chronic inflammation. At times when workers reported job satisfaction above their own personal average, they also showed lower perceived stress and lower chronic inflammation than at their own lower-satisfaction times. For employees without unpaid caregiving duties at home, higher job satisfaction was indirectly linked to lower inflammation through greater positive work-to-family spillover, and to lower perceived stress through reduced work-family conflict.

**Why it matters:** It links job satisfaction to a hard physiological health marker using within-person comparisons, strengthening the case that how people feel about their jobs is a health issue rather than just an HR metric.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Job satisfaction surveys are usually framed as engagement and retention tools; this suggests they also track something with biological consequences — and that employees juggling unpaid caregiving may not get the same spillover benefits from a good day at work.
- **For general readers:** A satisfying stretch at work appears to follow you home in measurable ways, but if you are also caring for a relative or child outside work hours, the buffering effect looks weaker.

**Caveats:** Single occupation and industry (nursing home workers), observational design that cannot prove job satisfaction causes lower inflammation, predictors and most outcomes self-reported, and caregiver-status differences were patterns rather than cleanly distinct pathways.

### 12. Constantly scanning for problems makes leaders feel impactful — and depleted at the same time

**Journal:** *Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-17
**Work ID:** W7213501285 | **DOI:** 10.1111/apps.70142

**The study:** Using an experience sampling study of 176 leader-follower dyads plus a scenario-based experiment (N=142), researchers tracked what happens to leaders themselves when they engage in vigilant leadership — actively monitoring for risks and problems. The behavior ran down two simultaneous paths: it raised leaders' sense of having impact at work, which boosted their engagement, while also producing ego depletion, which drained it. Followers' proactive personality tilted the balance: with proactive followers, the energizing path strengthened and the draining path weakened.

**Why it matters:** It reframes vigilance as a cost borne by the leader, and suggests the net toll depends heavily on who is on the team — leaders of passive teams pay more for the same behavior.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you're the manager always scanning for what could go wrong, notice that the drain is real and partly determined by your team: building followers who spot and act on problems themselves may be the most direct way to make vigilance sustainable for you.
- **For general readers:** The boss who seems exhausted by watching everything may be caught in a genuine bind — the same behavior that makes them feel they matter is what wears them out.

**Caveats:** The experience sampling study is short-term and self-report for leader states; the second study is a scenario-based experiment with hypothetical rather than real decisions and a modest N; ego depletion as a construct remains contested in the broader literature.

### 13. Being bossed by an algorithm may actually help gig workers switch off at home

**Journal:** *Journal of Managerial Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7204867954 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jmp-10-2025-1066

**The study:** Chinese researchers surveyed 658 gig workers across three time points to test how algorithmic management — platform systems that set goals, assign tasks and track performance — spills over into family life. Drawing on goal-setting theory, they found that tighter algorithmic direction increased workers' concentration on the job, which in turn was linked to more positive and less negative work-to-family spillover. The benefit faded for workers high in "depletion sensitivity," who tire quickly and found the same system draining.

**Why it matters:** Algorithmic management is usually cast as a purely dehumanizing force, but this suggests clear, system-set goals can reduce the mental residue workers carry home — while hurting the subset of people who exhaust easily.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For managers running platform or heavily tracked work: the study hints that the problem may be less automation per se than one-size-fits-all intensity. Worth examining whether your most fatigue-prone workers need adjustable targets or recovery buffers, rather than assuming monitoring affects everyone identically.
- **For general readers:** For anyone doing delivery, rideshare or freelance platform work: structure imposed from outside can make it easier to stop thinking about the job once the shift ends — but only if you're not already running on empty.

**Caveats:** Self-report survey at all three waves, so common-method bias is possible and causation can't be firmly established; gig workers in a single country; "positive spillover" measured by workers' own perceptions, not family members'; time lags were short.

### 14. When the economy breaks, employees start caring a lot more about whether pay is fair

**Journal:** *Human Resource Management Journal* | **Published:** 2026-08-07
**Work ID:** W7201985959 | **DOI:** 10.1111/1748-8583.70061

**The study:** Using repeated employee surveys collected between 2007 and 2011 at Japanese-owned subsidiaries in China, researchers tracked how the drivers of organizational commitment shifted around the global financial crisis. Across four subsidiaries, reliance on reward fairness rose in one manufacturing site; a focused analysis of production workers there showed that perceived fairness of rewards became a significantly stronger predictor of commitment after the crisis, while other commitment drivers stayed flat or weakened. The effect was notably weaker among supervisors than frontline employees.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that during downturns, the usual levers of loyalty lose power and distributive fairness becomes the one that matters most — especially for the people furthest from the decisions.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** In a cost-cutting or restructuring cycle, perks and development talk may do less for commitment than being able to show that pay, bonuses and cuts were allocated fairly. Managers should also note that their own reaction to fairness isn't a good proxy for their team's — supervisors in this study were far less sensitive to it.
- **For general readers:** Economic anxiety changes what employees watch. When jobs feel precarious, people stop evaluating their employer on vague goodwill and start counting who got what.

**Caveats:** Non-experimental repeated cross-sections, not a tracked panel of the same individuals; the key effect appeared in one manufacturing subsidiary rather than uniformly; Japanese-owned firms in China in a specific historical window; moderation effect for supervisors was borderline (p = 0.050).

### 15. "Responsible" leaders create two kinds of team harmony — and only one of them helps

**Journal:** *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7213349432 | **DOI:** 10.1111/joop.70157

**The study:** Across three studies, including multilevel, multisource, time-lagged data collected in more than one country, researchers developed measures of "genuine" versus "superficial" team harmony toward stakeholders and tested how responsible leadership — leadership oriented to stakeholders beyond shareholders — affects teams. Responsible leadership triggered both kinds of harmony: genuine harmony boosted team creativity and citizenship behavior toward stakeholders, while superficial harmony (smoothing things over for appearances) dragged them down. A mastery-oriented team climate amplified the good path and suppressed the bad one; a performance-oriented climate did the reverse.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a concrete failure mode for values-driven leadership: teams can perform agreement with the leader's stakeholder agenda instead of actually engaging with it, and a hard performance climate makes that fake version more likely.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you lead a team on ESG, customer or community commitments, watch for the difference between real buy-in and polite conformity. The study suggests emphasizing learning and mastery, rather than raw performance targets, shifts teams toward the genuine kind — worth testing in how you frame goals and run meetings.
- **For general readers:** Most people recognize the meeting where everyone nods and nothing changes. This research puts a name to it and shows why high-pressure environments breed it.

**Caveats:** Survey-based despite multisource and time-lagged design, so causal claims remain tentative; new self-developed harmony measures; team-level sample sizes not reported in the abstract; cultural generalizability still open.

### 16. Training budgets tell you almost nothing — how many people attend tells you who stays

**Journal:** *The International Journal of Human Resource Management* | **Published:** 2026-09-26
**Work ID:** W7214434160 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09585192.2026.2737821

**The study:** Researchers analyzed longitudinal data on Korean manufacturing firms with a two-year lagged design and generalized estimating equations, comparing training-cost measures against composite indicators that combine cost with training time, enrollment rate, and financial and HR "slack." Models based on the ratio of training cost to HR slack best explained firms' financial performance two years later, while training cost multiplied by enrollment rate best predicted lower turnover. Training spend on its own was the weakest way to evaluate the investment.

**Why it matters:** Most firms report training as a cost line, which this evidence says is close to meaningless; reach and spare capacity to absorb it are what connect training to money and retention.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you own an L&D budget, change what you report upward: participation breadth and whether the organization has the HR headroom to apply new skills predict outcomes better than dollars per head. A big spend concentrated on a few people, in a team with no slack, looks like investment and behaves like nothing.
- **For general readers:** For employees, it explains a familiar frustration: companies boast about training spend while most staff never get access, or come back from a course to a workload that leaves no room to use it.

**Caveats:** Firm-level archival data from one country and sector (Korean manufacturing), so mechanisms at the individual level are inferred; correlational despite the two-year lag; indicators are proxies, with no measure of training content or quality.

### 17. Trained workers job-hunt less — because training makes the job feel like a fit

**Journal:** *Personnel Review* | **Published:** 2026-08-12
**Work ID:** W7202297645 | **DOI:** 10.1108/pr-07-2024-0659

**The study:** Drawing on a national survey of Chinese social work agencies, researchers analyzed 2,971 community workers nested in 215 agencies across 41 regions. Employer-provided training was associated with less job-search activity, and work engagement explained much of that link. The dampening effect of training on job search was stronger in regions where agencies imitated peers or followed professional norms, and weaker where training was driven by government mandate.

**Why it matters:** It suggests training retains people mainly by making the current job more engaging — and that compliance-driven, box-ticking training does not produce the same effect.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Before expanding a training program, ask why it exists. This study indicates that training adopted because it is professionally expected or copied from respected peers holds people better than training run to satisfy a regulator — a useful question for anyone defending an L&D line item.
- **For general readers:** The common worry that training makes staff more marketable and likelier to leave didn't hold here; workers who got development were less likely to be looking elsewhere.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so causation can't be established and engaged workers may simply receive more training; a single occupation (community/social workers) in one country; job search is self-reported, not actual turnover.

### 18. Politically skilled employees aren't spared abusive bosses — their extra effort is what protects them

**Journal:** *Journal of Managerial Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-08-05
**Work ID:** W7172502435 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jmp-07-2025-0664

**The study:** A three-wave survey of 224 employees at a Chinese transportation firm tested whether subordinates' political skill shields them from abusive supervision. Political skill had no direct relationship with being mistreated; instead, politically skilled employees did more organization-directed citizenship behavior, and that extra discretionary effort was what predicted less abuse. Task performance, notably, did not serve the same protective function.

**Why it matters:** It reverses the usual framing in which employee behavior is only an outcome of abusive leadership, suggesting instead that what subordinates visibly contribute shapes how supervisors treat them.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Uncomfortable but useful: simply hitting your targets may not register with a hostile manager, while visible contributions beyond the job description do. Managers should flip the lens and ask whether they are unconsciously reserving harsher treatment for people who do their job and nothing more.
- **For general readers:** The finding speaks to a familiar office dynamic — being good at reading people pays off not through charm alone, but through knowing which contributions get noticed.

**Caveats:** Single firm, single industry, single country; modest sample (N=224); all measures self-reported across the three waves, limiting causal claims about the direction between abuse and employee behavior; abusive supervision rated by subordinates only.

### 19. "Career success" means different things depending on what's driving you

**Journal:** *Journal of Career Assessment* | **Published:** 2026-09-04
**Work ID:** W7208791578 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10690727261485957

**The study:** In a time-separated survey of 377 U.S. women, researchers used canonical correlation analysis to map the two dimensions of a protean career — being self-directed and being values-driven — onto eight distinct facets of career success rather than a single satisfaction score. Growth and development was the facet most strongly tied to protean attitudes overall, and recognition the least. Self-directed women linked success most to growth and satisfaction; values-driven women linked it most to personal life.

**Why it matters:** It shows that collapsing career success into "are you satisfied?" hides what people are actually chasing — and that two people both described as having protean careers may want entirely different things.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For managers holding career conversations, the practical message is to stop assuming promotions and recognition are the universal currency. Asking whether someone is optimizing for growth, for alignment with their values, or for room for a personal life changes what a meaningful retention offer looks like.
- **For general readers:** For anyone taking stock of their own career: external recognition came out as the weakest correlate of a self-authored career, which may be reassuring to people who feel they are doing well by their own standards but not collecting titles.

**Caveats:** Correlational and exploratory, with no causal or longitudinal test despite the time separation; U.S. women only, so comparisons across gender aren't possible; one success dimension (authenticity) had to be dropped for poor discriminant validity; all self-report.

### 20. When the boss brags about being swamped, your read on why decides what happens next

**Journal:** *Journal of Organizational Behavior* | **Published:** 2026-08-12
**Work ID:** W7202354404 | **DOI:** 10.1002/job.70124

**The study:** Two studies examined how employees respond to leaders who boast about how stressed and overloaded they are: a scenario-based experiment with 294 employees and a two-wave survey of 332 employees. When subordinates attributed the stress bragging to a genuine push for performance, they engaged in task-focused reflection and reported higher work engagement. When they read it as impression management, they ruminated about the leader instead and withdrew from interacting with them.

**Why it matters:** Leaders who narrate their own exhaustion may think they are modeling commitment, but the same words can either energize a team or quietly push people away depending on the motive employees infer.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers can audit their own "I was up until 2am on this" talk: the study suggests the deciding factor is whether the comment is tied to the work and the standard, or to the leader's image. The experimental half shows what can happen in principle, so the practical test is to ask trusted colleagues how such remarks actually land on the team.
- **For general readers:** A field guide to the boss who is always drowning: whether their busyness talk motivates you or makes you avoid them says as much about the trust in the relationship as about the workload.

**Caveats:** Study 1 is a hypothetical scenario experiment rather than real work decisions; Study 2 is a two-wave self-report survey with predictor and outcome from the same source, limiting causal claims and raising common-method concerns.

### 21. Cadets who were happy at work two years earlier were likelier to win scarce leadership posts

**Journal:** *International Journal of Selection and Assessment* | **Published:** 2026-09-11
**Work ID:** W7212286790 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ijsa.70083

**The study:** Researchers followed 444 first- and second-year cadets at the Korea Military Academy who reported their happiness at work in 2023, then checked who was appointed to competitive leadership roles in their fourth year — positions held by roughly 4% of each cohort. Controlling for baseline cumulative performance, gender and cohort, higher early happiness at work predicted selection (odds ratio 1.68). Affective organizational commitment was the component that most clearly predicted selection; job satisfaction did not.

**Why it matters:** Even in a system explicitly built to rank people on measurable merit, how attached someone feels to the organization early on appears to shape who later gets handed authority.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If emotional attachment predicts promotion above and beyond performance scores, organizations should ask whether their selection processes are quietly rewarding enthusiasm and fit — and whether that advantages people who were already well treated. Worth checking in your own promotion data whether engagement surveys forecast advancement independent of appraisal ratings.
- **For general readers:** The long game of career advancement may start earlier and softer than people expect: feeling committed to where you work in year one can show up in who gets picked in year four.

**Caveats:** Single institution in one country (a military academy with unusual hierarchy and selection rules), modest N for a rare outcome, confidence intervals near the significance boundary for several components, and the correlational design cannot rule out that happier cadets differed in unmeasured ways.

### 22. Organizational support only pays off in performance when the manager converts it into motivation

**Journal:** *Journal of Leadership & Organizational Studies* | **Published:** 2026-08-10
**Work ID:** W7202072432 | **DOI:** 10.1177/15480518261475717

**The study:** Using multisource, multi-time data from 217 employees and 41 supervisors, researchers tested why perceived organizational support sometimes translates into better in-role performance and sometimes doesn't. Support predicted supervisor-rated performance through employees' sense of competence and self-determination. Crucially, those indirect paths were significant only when transformational leadership was high — when it was low, the support-to-empowerment link essentially disappeared.

**Why it matters:** Companies invest heavily in support-style HR practices (benefits, recognition, development), and this suggests the return on that spending depends on whether immediate managers help people experience the support as empowerment.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Before adding another support program, managers can ask whether frontline leaders are doing the translation work; the study implies HR investments and leadership development are complements, not substitutes. A natural internal test is comparing engagement and performance outcomes of the same benefits package across teams with differing manager behavior.
- **For general readers:** It helps explain a familiar frustration: why generous company perks can feel hollow under one boss and genuinely motivating under another.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional in structure despite multi-time collection, so causation is not established; relatively small sample (217 employees, 41 supervisors) and empowerment measures are self-reported; context of a single organizational setting limits generalization.

### 23. Employees sent abroad come home to a company that has moved on — and many start looking elsewhere

**Journal:** *The International Journal of Human Resource Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215123713 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09585192.2026.2740757

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 35 repatriates across industries, plus focus groups with HR professionals, about returning from international assignments during geopolitical crises and COVID-19. They distinguish career-function support (career continuity, making foreign knowledge usable, structured reintegration) from identity-belonging support (recognition, managerial empathy, inclusion in peer networks). Repatriates returning during disruption were acutely sensitive to gaps between what was promised before departure and what they actually got, and those gaps tracked with lower well-being, weaker commitment and intentions to leave.

**Why it matters:** International assignments are expensive talent investments, and this suggests the losses happen at the return, when the organization treats reentry as an administrative formality rather than a re-belonging process.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you manage returning expatriates, the actionable point is that a job slot alone is not reintegration: recognition of what they learned abroad and reentry into informal networks mattered as much as the role itself. Audit the promises made at departure against what reentering staff actually experience.
- **For general readers:** Anyone who has returned from a secondment, long leave or relocation will recognize the quiet shock of coming back to a workplace whose social map has redrawn itself without them.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interview study with 35 participants — no quantitative effect estimates, no comparison group, and self-reported retrospective accounts across mixed industries; findings are exploratory and not generalizable in a statistical sense.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch independently undercut the instruments organizations use to decide who is good at their job. "The IQ test's grip on hiring just got weaker" (W7203815879) cuts cognitive ability's validity from roughly .50 to .20 and finds it does *not* track cognitive job complexity; "When men's scores vary more, the selection test picks a workforce that's both mostly male and weaker" (W7207756322) shows top-down score selection can degrade both diversity and expected performance; the self-monitoring meta-analysis (W7202110265) finds social adaptability moves supervisor ratings but not objective output; and the Korea Military Academy study (W7212286790) finds early organizational commitment predicted scarce leadership appointments beyond measured performance. Taken together: both ends of the merit pipeline — the test at the door and the rating that decides promotion — are measuring something other than what employers think.

**Why pitch this now:** Employers are buying AI-scored assessments and "skills-based hiring" tools at speed while simultaneously defending selection decisions in a hostile legal and political climate around diversity — and the central validity number those products were sold on has just been revised down by more than half.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the quiet collapse of confidence in workplace measurement — how an industry built on the claim that it can rank people objectively is finding that its best tools explain a few percent of performance, reward polish over output, and can be re-engineered so that fairness and quality improve together. Works for a business/management audience wanting to audit its own hiring stack, and for general readers who have been ranked by one of these systems.

**Potential headlines:**
- The Hiring Test That Was Supposed to Predict Performance Explains About 4 Percent of It
- Everything Your Employer Measures About You Rewards the Wrong Thing
- You Were Judged by a Score. The Science Behind It Just Shrank.

**Potential outlets:**
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — its readership buys and defends assessment systems; a piece that hands them the revised validity numbers and the selection-design fixes from W7207756322 is directly actionable.
- **The Atlantic** — long-running interest in meritocracy's self-justifications; this is the empirical version of that argument, with the variability-and-selection modeling as the fresh hook.
- **Wired** — covers algorithmic hiring and assessment vendors; the story that the underlying human-science baseline has been revised downward reframes its AI-screening coverage.
- **Business Insider** — strong careers and recruiting desk with a job-seeker readership that has personally been filtered by aptitude tests and appraisal ratings.

### Study 1: When she out-earns him, her work-family conflict rises — his doesn't
**Work ID:** W7203464894 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Post hoc analysis presented as established finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the research shows the strain spikes at modest deviations from the traditional arrangement — the moment a woman starts earning a bit more — then levels off as the gap widens"
- **Abstract says:** "Post hoc analyses suggest a front‐loaded pattern, with women's WFC increasing most sharply at modest deviations from traditional earning arrangements and leveling off as pay gaps widen."
- **Problem:** The abstract explicitly labels this as a post hoc, suggestive pattern; the digest reports it as something "the research shows," giving it more evidentiary weight than the authors claim.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "post hoc analyses suggest the strain rises most sharply at modest deviations...""

**Issue B. Causal wording for the male null result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "for men, the pay gap had no such effect"
- **Abstract says:** "Results show that larger partner pay gaps are associated with higher WFC for women, but not for men."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports associations in observational panel data; "had no such effect" implies a tested causal relationship.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "for men, no such association appeared.""

### Study 3: Feeling excluded at work ruins your evening — and you wake up worse for it
**Work ID:** W7220972684 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal language for within-person diary associations** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Days when people felt more included in their workgroup led to more positive and less negative evening reflection about work, which in turn predicted better well-being the next morning."
- **Abstract says:** "multilevel path analyses supported the proposed model, demonstrating that positive and negative evening work reflection explain how daily workgroup inclusion carries over to next‐morning well‐being"
- **Problem:** "Led to" asserts causation from a single-source daily diary study; the digest's own caveat states causation cannot be established, so the body contradicts the caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "Days when people felt more included were followed by more positive and less negative evening reflection, which in turn was associated with better well-being the next morning.""

### Study 7: Tech-savvy employees burn out faster when the organization is disorganized
**Work ID:** W7214892649 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Claimed benefit under supportive conditions** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Readiness only translated into lower strain when organizational structures and leadership norms backed it up."
- **Abstract says:** "TR is associated with higher levels of emotional exhaustion if it is not supported by formalization and TMTBC."
- **Problem:** The abstract only reports the harmful association when support is absent; it does not state that readiness is associated with lower exhaustion when formalization and TMT boundary control are present.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "The strain association only disappeared when organizational structures and leadership norms backed readiness up.""

**Issue B. Unsupported mechanism (extra digital demands)** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Companies investing in digital upskilling may be inadvertently loading their most capable people with more digital demands"
- **Abstract says:** "we conceptualize TR as a personal resource and then develop and test a multilevel model in which its relationship with emotional exhaustion depends on unit-level formalization and organizational-level top management team boundary control (TMTBC)"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not test or report that tech-ready employees take on more digital demands; the mechanism is the digest's inference.
- **Suggested fix:** "Mark it as a hypothesis, e.g. "one possible explanation the study does not test is that...""

### Study 8: Having a boss or mentor of your own gender feels better — but doesn't improve outcomes
**Work ID:** W7204030144 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Dropped comparison group in the junior-women finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Junior women reported more perceived similarity and support in gender-similar dyads"
- **Abstract says:** "junior women report higher perceived similarity and support in gender similar dyads than men"
- **Problem:** The abstract's comparison is junior women versus junior men within gender-similar dyads; the digest reads as a comparison of similar versus dissimilar dyads for women.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add the reference group: "Junior women reported more perceived similarity and support in gender-similar dyads than junior men did.""

**Issue B. Career-outcome claim not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the accumulated evidence suggests the relationship's quality matters more for your career than the demographic match"
- **Abstract says:** "Findings reveal that gender similarity promotes perceived similarity and relationship quality, but not work‐related outcomes."
- **Problem:** The meta-analysis examined gender similarity as the predictor; it did not test whether relationship quality predicts career outcomes, so the comparative claim goes beyond the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say instead that gender matching was not linked to work outcomes in this evidence base, without ranking it against relationship quality."

### Study 11: More cultural intelligence isn't always better: a study of expatriates in Abu Dhabi finds the facets can work against each other
**Work ID:** W7211941550 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal verb for cross-sectional associations** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Overall CQ predicted all three performance ratings, and it was more strongly tied to citizenship behaviors than to core task performance."
- **Abstract says:** "Global CQ was related to all performance measures, but the relationships of the four facets of CQ differed across measures... global CQ was more strongly related to OCB-H and OCB-C than to task performance"
- **Problem:** The abstract uses purely associational language ("related to") for what the digest itself describes as a cross-sectional survey; "predicted" implies temporal or causal precedence the design cannot support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "Overall CQ was associated with all three performance ratings"."

**Issue B. Interactions described as post hoc** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the counterintuitive interactions are post hoc and need replication"
- **Abstract says:** "Of special interest were the interactions between meta-cognitive CQ (MCQ) with the other three facets of CQ... The first study with specifically collected data to test the interactions between CQ facets"
- **Problem:** The abstract frames the facet interactions as the study's a priori focus with data collected specifically to test them, not as post hoc exploration; the caveat mischaracterizes the design.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "the interaction patterns were more nuanced than the authors expected and need replication"."

### Study 12: Teams that believe they can deliver don't just do more — they share the load more evenly
**Work ID:** W7203699710 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Quantity vs. quality of contributions** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Teams that believe they can deliver don't just do more — they share the load more evenly"
- **Abstract says:** "These findings highlight team potency as an internal driver of both higher-quality and more equal contributions within teams."
- **Problem:** The abstract characterizes the level effect as "higher-quality" contributions; the digest translates this as doing "more," a quantity claim the abstract does not make explicit beyond the team-level mean of task contributions.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "contribute more strongly" or mirror the abstract's "higher-quality and more equal contributions"."

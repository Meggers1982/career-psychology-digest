### Study 1: CEOs quit at the top and at the bottom — it's the middling performers who hang on
**Work ID:** W7223031859 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Goal orientation types specified beyond abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Executives' own goal orientations (whether they were chasing mastery or avoiding failure) shifted which mechanism dominated."
- **Abstract says:** "We further consider executives’ goal orientations as boundary conditions to probe the underlying mechanisms."
- **Problem:** The abstract names "goal orientations" as boundary conditions but never specifies mastery versus avoidance dimensions, so the parenthetical detail is unverifiable from the abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write "Executives' own achievement goal orientations acted as boundary conditions, shifting which mechanism dominated," without naming specific orientation types."

### Study 2: Jobs that are moderately complex — not simple or hard — make people most likely to want out
**Work ID:** W7222847257 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Moderation by sense of power simplified** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Employees with a stronger workplace sense of power felt less threatened overall."
- **Abstract says:** "Workplace sense of power was associated with lower threat and a more pronounced inverted-U association."
- **Problem:** The digest reports only the main effect and drops the second half of the moderation — that the curvilinear pattern was *stronger*, not weaker, for employees high in sense of power. The story angle ("Giving those employees more voice and influence may blunt the identity threat") rests on the dropped half and may point the wrong way for mid-complexity jobs.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that a stronger sense of power was linked to lower threat overall but also to a more pronounced inverted-U pattern, and soften the managerial recommendation accordingly."

**Issue B. Study 1 scope overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Both found an inverted-U pattern: occupational identity threat — the feeling that your work no longer confirms who you are professionally — peaked at intermediate complexity, and that threat in turn predicted intention to switch careers."
- **Abstract says:** "Study 1 analyzed ... 43,859 employees in 29 European countries to examine the focal association. Study 2 used two-wave survey data from 434 Chinese full-time employees to test the full research model."
- **Problem:** Only Study 2 tested the full model including career change intention; Study 1 tested the complexity–threat association alone. The sentence implies both studies covered the intention outcome.
- **Suggested fix:** "Clarify that Study 1 established the complexity–threat curve and Study 2 tested the full path through to career change intention."

### Study 4: For immigrants, the cultural potluck may be the best career networking event in town
**Work ID:** W7223147797 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unsupported comparative claim** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the connection that gets you hired is more likely to come from a cultural or social gathering than from a resume workshop"
- **Abstract says:** "One surprising finding was that nonwork-focused activities were related to numerous career benefits. For example, multiple regression results showed immigrants’ engagement in the CBOs’ cultural/social activities was significantly related to receiving career sponsorship, work-related assistance, social support, job search assistance, protection and political guidance, and information and ideas."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that nonwork activities were also related to career benefits; it does not report that they outperformed work-focused programming, and the cross-sectional design cannot establish that any activity "gets you hired."
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "the social side of community groups is linked to the same career benefits as the explicitly work-focused side" and drop the hiring-causation wording; soften the headline's "best" accordingly."

### Study 5: Pressure only turns into proactive work when employees have the inner resources to absorb it
**Work ID:** W7223306094 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Null effect overstated for performance pressure** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "For those lacking that combination, the pressure did not convert into constructive adaptation."
- **Abstract says:** "the positive association between performance pressure and task crafting was significantly stronger among employees exhibiting high levels of mindfulness and self-efficacy. Similarly, citizenship pressure predicted relational crafting only when these personal resources were concurrently high."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a conditional "only when" result for citizenship pressure but only a "significantly stronger" effect for performance pressure — not an absent one.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the performance-pressure link was markedly weaker (not absent) without both resources, while citizenship pressure only worked when both were high."

### Study 7: How employees explain who got promoted predicts how engaged they are — and women and younger staff tell a different story
**Work ID:** W7221331977 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Profile stability misstated** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Three stable patterns emerged: meritocratic attributors (it's about competence), institutional attributors (it's about rules and procedures), and relational-structural attributors (it's about networks and gender dynamics)."
- **Abstract says:** "Study 2 shows that the meritocratic attributors profile and the relational-structural attributors profile are both stable over time, while the institutional attributors profile is more fluid."
- **Problem:** Only two of the three profiles were longitudinally stable; the institutional profile was explicitly described as more fluid, so calling all three "stable" misreports a key result.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write "Three patterns emerged ... the meritocratic and relational-structural profiles were stable over time, while the institutional profile was more fluid.""

### Study 9: Career skills predict feeling successful later — but only if you act on them
**Work ID:** W7222470451 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Competency dimensions not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Career competencies — knowing yourself, knowing the market, and networking and self-profiling skills — predicted several facets of subjective career success over time"
- **Abstract says:** "longitudinal results from Study 2 revealed that career competencies predict multiple facets of subjective career success, with career crafting serving as a behavioral enactment."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not enumerate the CCQ's dimensions, so the three named competencies are unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop the dash-list of dimensions or attribute it explicitly to the CCQ instrument rather than to this paper's reported findings."

**Issue B. Mediation language slightly firm** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "with "career crafting" (actually taking steps to shape one's career) acting as the behavioral channel through which competencies translated into success"
- **Abstract says:** "with career crafting serving as a behavioral enactment"
- **Problem:** "Behavioral enactment" is weaker than a tested mediating channel; the three-wave design supports temporal ordering, not a demonstrated causal pathway.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say career crafting "served as the behavioral enactment linking competencies to reported success," keeping the association framing."

### Study 11: Expats who adapt well do better abroad — and the bigger the culture gap, the more it pays off
**Work ID:** W7213985838 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Mildly causal headline for cross-sectional data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Expats who adapt well do better abroad — and the bigger the culture gap, the more it pays off"
- **Abstract says:** "Using survey data from 325 expatriates and multiple linear regression analyses, we test direct and moderated relationships. The results show that career adaptability is positively associated with health, happiness, and productivity."
- **Problem:** "do better" and "pays off" read as effects of adaptability, whereas the abstract reports only associations from a single-wave survey. The body text and caveats are appropriately hedged, so this is confined to the headline.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to an association, e.g. "Expats who adapt well report better health, happiness and productivity — most so when the culture gap is wide"."

### Study 1: After a team flops, flat teams rebound by recruiting a star — hierarchical ones won't
**Work ID:** W7203807114 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Resisted" overstates a null effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Teams with steep hierarchies saw the same recruit as a personal threat and resisted adding them."
- **Abstract says:** "failure will increase the willingness to add high-status members in teams with low but not high status differentiation"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that failure did not increase willingness in high-differentiation teams, which is a null effect, not an active reduction or resistance. Members perceiving a threat is the theorized mechanism, not a measured behavioral refusal.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as "Teams with steep hierarchies showed no such increase in willingness, with members viewing the addition as a threat to their own status.""

**Issue B. Lab description of the team experiment** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the experimental teams were short-lived lab groups performing assigned tasks rather than ongoing workplace teams"
- **Abstract says:** "two preregistered experiments at the individual (Study 2, n = 783 participants) and team levels (Study 3, n = 222 teams)"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not describe the setting, duration or task of the team-level experiment, so this characterization is unverifiable from the abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "the experiments were short-term preregistered studies rather than studies of ongoing workplace teams (setting not specified in the abstract).""

### Study 2: Gossip is how new hires actually learn the rules — if it's true, work-related and interesting
**Work ID:** W7207711849 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "All three" drops the abstract's hedge** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "All three attributes predicted clearer understanding of workplace norms and better social integration, over and above gossip frequency"
- **Abstract says:** "The results largely supported our hypotheses, showing that, above and beyond the frequency of gossip received, the three attributes of gossip content enhanced newcomers' norm clarity and social integration."
- **Problem:** "Largely supported" signals that not every predicted path held; the digest presents a uniform result for all three attributes on both outcomes.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add "largely" or "most of" — e.g. "The attributes largely predicted clearer norm understanding and better social integration...""

**Issue B. Causal and comparative phrasing in story angle** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "truthful work-relevant gossip helped newcomers get productive and committed faster than sheer volume of chatter"
- **Abstract says:** "above and beyond the frequency of gossip received, the three attributes of gossip content enhanced newcomers' norm clarity and social integration. In turn, norm clarity increased task proficiency, while social integration improved affective commitment."
- **Problem:** This is an observational experience-sampling study, so "helped" implies causation, and "faster" implies a speed comparison the study did not test (content was tested over and above frequency, not against it on a timeline).
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "gossip content, not just how much newcomers heard, was associated with norm clarity and integration, which in turn tracked with proficiency and commitment.""

### Study 6: What male allies actually do at work — and why managers' motives differ from everyone else's
**Work ID:** W7214919069 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Shared motives dropped** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Managers were more strongly motivated by job-related reasons, while non-managers more often cited empathic concern for women coworkers"
- **Abstract says:** "Although both managers and non-managers were motivated by moral obligation and social justice concerns, managers were more strongly motivated by job-related motives, whereas non-managers more often emphasized empathic concern for women coworkers."
- **Problem:** Omitting the common baseline of moral obligation and social justice makes the manager/non-manager difference look like a wholesale divergence in motive, which feeds the story angle's suggestion that managers' motives are career-driven.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that both groups cited moral obligation and social justice, with the job-related vs. empathic difference layered on top."

**Issue B. Typology also covers recommended behaviors** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "open-ended critical incident reports from 85 working men (45 managers, 40 non-managers) describing their real allyship experiences"
- **Abstract says:** "a typology of enacted and recommended allyship behaviours"
- **Problem:** The typology includes behaviors participants recommended, not only ones they actually enacted, which the digest's "real allyship experiences" framing obscures.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that the typology combines behaviors men reported enacting with ones they recommended."

### Study 7: The team member who organizes everyone else learns the least from team training
**Work ID:** W7202035170 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Team learning moderation overgeneralized** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "strong team learning amplified the pattern"
- **Abstract says:** "we find support for the amplifying top-down influence of team learning effectiveness on the relationships between Doer and Organizer orientations and learning outcomes"
- **Problem:** The amplifying effect was found for the Doer and Organizer orientations only, not for the full set of relationships including Team Builder; "the pattern" implies it applied across the board.
- **Suggested fix:** "Specify "team learning effectiveness amplified the Doer and Organizer effects.""

**Issue B. Immediate measurement claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "learning outcomes measured immediately with no evidence of transfer to actual jobs"
- **Abstract says:** "From a sample of 84 student teams (N = 330) engaged in a simulation-based team training program..."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not state the timing of the learning measures, so the "measured immediately" detail is unverifiable from the abstract only (the no-transfer-to-jobs point is fair given the student sample).
- **Suggested fix:** "Trim to "learning outcomes were measured within the simulation, with no evidence of transfer to actual jobs.""

### Study 8: Staying quiet in your first weeks can make you a target
**Work ID:** W7215049715 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Protective effect overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "newcomers with higher social status in the group were largely shielded, even when seen as submissive"
- **Abstract says:** "newcomer social status served as the protective factor that could protect silent newcomers who were perceived as submissive from being hazed"
- **Problem:** The abstract describes status as a buffering moderator, not as near-complete protection; "largely shielded" implies a stronger attenuation than reported.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "higher social status buffered silent newcomers from hazing.""

**Issue B. Measurement sourcing and timing not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "at two time points, pairing new hires' reports of their own silence with their coworkers' perceptions and behavior"
- **Abstract says:** "We conducted a time-lagged survey involving 266 newcomer–coworker dyads in mainland China."
- **Problem:** The abstract confirms a time-lagged dyadic design but does not specify two waves or which variables came from which member of the dyad; this allocation is unverifiable from the abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "a time-lagged dyadic survey of 266 newcomer–coworker pairs" without assigning specific measures to each source."

### Study 9: When a colleague redesigns their own job, coworkers either copy them or sabotage them
**Work ID:** W7205707681 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline mischaracterizes the measured outcome** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "When a colleague redesigns their own job, coworkers either copy them or sabotage them"
- **Abstract says:** "observer functional behavioural responses (i.e., more helping and less undermining) and dysfunctional behavioural responses (i.e., less helping and more undermining)"
- **Problem:** The positive outcome measured was helping behavior toward the crafter, not imitating their job crafting, so "copy them" names an outcome the study did not assess.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "coworkers either pitch in or undercut them.""

**Issue B. Causal verbs for correlational data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Seeing a coworker craft their job sparked inspiration, which led to more helping and less undermining — but it also sparked anxiety, which led to more social undermining."
- **Abstract says:** "inspiration mediated the relationship between coworker job crafting and both helping behaviour and social undermining, while anxiety only mediated the relationship between coworker job crafting and social undermining"
- **Problem:** The abstract consistently uses associative language for a three-wave survey and an experience-sampling study; "sparked" and "led to" read as established causation, which sits awkwardly against the digest's own caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "was associated with" / "in turn related to" in the body text."

### Study 10: When a coworker treats you badly, you start blaming your boss too
**Work ID:** W7205007222 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal phrasing for predictive findings** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Being treated unfairly by a coworker led employees to blame that coworker and then, in sequence, to blame and perceive injustice from their supervisor."
- **Abstract says:** "event-based data demonstrate that coworker injustice predicts supervisor injustice through the sequential mediation of coworker and supervisor blame"
- **Problem:** The abstract says "predicts" for event-based data; "led to" asserts causation that the described designs (and the digest's own caveat about same-source self-report) do not support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "Coworker injustice predicted blame toward the coworker and, in sequence, blame and perceived injustice toward the supervisor.""

**Issue B. Boundary condition simplified** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "strongest when employees saw the offending coworker as acting with the supervisor's delegated authority while also holding higher informal status than the victim did"
- **Abstract says:** "perceived intermediary delegation (PID)… strengthens the blame migrating effect when victims concurrently hold lower relative statuses with their supervisors than their offending coworkers"
- **Problem:** The abstract frames the status condition in terms of the victim's status relative to the supervisor compared with the coworker's; the digest's simpler "higher status than the victim" loses the relative-to-supervisor comparison.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that the effect peaked when the victim's standing with the supervisor was lower than the offending coworker's."

### Study 11: Plan for small changes, improvise through big ones
**Work ID:** W7214778895 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Goal orientation types not specified in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Teams' goal orientations — learning versus performance focus — predicted which process they actually engaged in."
- **Abstract says:** "We also find that team goal orientations differentially shape teams’ engagement in each process."
- **Problem:** The abstract refers only to "team goal orientations" without naming learning versus performance orientation, and does not say which orientation maps onto which adaptation process. The digest's specificity cannot be confirmed from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write "Teams' goal orientations shaped which process they engaged in (the abstract does not specify which orientation led to which)" or flag the learning/performance labels as unverifiable from the abstract."

**Issue B. "Decision rule" overstates a single study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It gives leaders a decision rule for reorganizations and disruptions"
- **Abstract says:** "These findings offer a more integrative account of team adaptation following change."
- **Problem:** The authors frame the contribution as a theoretical account, not a managerial decision rule; presenting one lab experiment plus one field survey as a rule for reorganizations leaps past what the abstract claims.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "It points leaders toward a contingency: planning helps for routine shifts, while improvisation may fit genuinely novel disruptions.""

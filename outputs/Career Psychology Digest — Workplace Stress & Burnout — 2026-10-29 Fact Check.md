### Study 1: Autonomy protects older workers from burnout more than it protects the young
**Work ID:** W7212031246 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Age moderation of engagement omitted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The protective link with burnout was significantly weaker in samples whose average age was under 35 than in later-career samples, while national individualism and power distance made no difference at all."
- **Abstract says:** "Moderation analyses indicated that both relationships were slightly stronger among older employees. Supplementary analyses further showed that this moderation effect was not continuous. The relationship between job autonomy and burnout was significantly weaker for early-career employees (samples with a mean age under 35) than for later-career employees."
- **Problem:** The digest reports only the burnout moderation, which may leave readers thinking age did not moderate the autonomy–engagement link at all; the abstract says both relationships were slightly stronger among older employees (though only burnout showed the significant early- vs later-career split).
- **Suggested fix:** "Add a clause: "Both relationships were slightly stronger among older employees, but only the autonomy–burnout link showed a significant early- vs later-career difference.""

**Issue B. Cross-sectional claim not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Meta-analysis of mostly cross-sectional primary studies, so causal direction is unclear"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract does not describe the design of the included primary studies."
- **Problem:** The characterization of the underlying studies as "mostly cross-sectional" cannot be verified from the abstract alone, even though the causal caution itself is appropriate.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "Meta-analysis of correlational data, so causal direction is unclear" unless the primary-study designs are confirmed in the full text."

### Study 2: Five ways people settle into a new job — and the one that goes badly
**Work ID:** W7223449472 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Outcome specifics attributed to the worst profile** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "those who focused only on mastering the task had the worst outcomes, including higher emotional exhaustion and turnover intentions"
- **Abstract says:** "The worst profile was driven solely by task socialization. ... Profile membership was associated with different patterns of adaptation in terms of work-related outcomes (i.e., performance, turnover intentions, job engagement, emotional exhaustion)"
- **Problem:** The abstract names exhaustion and turnover intentions only as outcomes studied; it does not state the direction of these outcomes for the Ill-Adapted Task-Driven profile, and the "worst profile" label is applied to global socialization experiences.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the task-only profile was the least adaptive overall and that profile membership was linked to differing patterns on performance, turnover intentions, engagement and exhaustion, without assigning specific directions."

**Issue B. 'Rather than performance' inference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "task-only onboarding is the single worst route, predicting exhaustion and exit rather than performance"
- **Abstract says:** "Profile membership was associated with different patterns of adaptation in terms of work-related outcomes (i.e., performance, turnover intentions, job engagement, emotional exhaustion)"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports associations only; it does not say the task-only path failed to deliver performance, and "predicting" leans causal for an observational latent-profile design.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "task-only onboarding was the least adaptive pathway and was associated with less favourable adaptation outcomes.""

**Issue C. Selective reporting of commitment predictors** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Profile membership was linked to commitment to supervisor and coworkers specifically, pointing to manager and buddy relationships as levers."
- **Abstract says:** "Newcomers' workplace affective commitment, specifically organization, task, supervisor and coworker commitments, were positively associated with membership into more desirable socialization profiles, albeit distinctively."
- **Problem:** Four commitment foci were examined (organization, task, supervisor, coworker) and all were positively associated with more desirable profiles; singling out supervisor and coworker as "specifically" misrepresents the finding, and the abstract treats commitment as a predictor of profile membership rather than a correlate of it.
- **Suggested fix:** "State that commitment to the organization, task, supervisor and coworkers were all positively associated with membership in more desirable profiles."

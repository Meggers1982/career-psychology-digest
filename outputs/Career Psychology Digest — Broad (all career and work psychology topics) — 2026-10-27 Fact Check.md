### Study 1: It's not which evening activity you choose — it's the mix
**Work ID:** W7169828568 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Antecedent finding stated as result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and were predicted by what had happened at work that day"
- **Abstract says:** "we assessed to what extent these profiles replicated in an independent sample and extended the investigation by including work-related antecedents and next-day work outcomes. Results indicated that daily recovery activities cluster together into identifiable profiles that largely replicated across samples. The identified profiles exhibited unique relationships with recovery experiences, well-being, and work outcomes."
- **Problem:** The abstract lists work-related antecedents as part of the design but reports results only for recovery experiences, well-being and work outcomes. Presenting the antecedent link as an established finding goes slightly beyond what the (truncated) abstract states.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to 'the study also examined whether what happened at work that day predicted which profile employees fell into' or note that the abstract does not report that result."

**Issue B. Specific recovery experiences unverifiable** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "linked to different recovery experiences (such as detachment and relaxation)"
- **Abstract says:** "The identified profiles exhibited unique relationships with recovery experiences, well-being, and work outcomes."
- **Problem:** Detachment and relaxation are plausible standard measures but are not named in the abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop the parenthetical examples or mark them as typical recovery-experience dimensions rather than ones the abstract names."

### Study 2: When the boss talks about their own good deeds, half the team is inspired and half is repelled
**Work ID:** W7167868566 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies a 50/50 split** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "half the team is inspired and half is repelled"
- **Abstract says:** "supervisor self-focused moral capitalization was positively related to employee moral elevation and, in turn, increased citizenship. However, for supervisors who engaged in less other-focused moral capitalization, supervisor self-focused moral capitalization instead triggered employee disgust and decreased citizenship behavior."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports no proportions, and the split is not between employees within a team but conditional on whether the supervisor also credits others. The headline suggests half of any given team reacts badly.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe to the actual boundary condition, e.g. 'inspiring — unless the boss never credits anyone else'."

**Issue B. Experiment design detail unverifiable** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the supporting experiment is relatively small (N=233 across conditions) and uses a controlled scenario"
- **Abstract says:** "an experiment involving 233 employees"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not say the experiment used a scenario/vignette design. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say 'an experiment with 233 employees' without specifying the manipulation format, or verify from the full text."

### Study 6: Leaders take more risks — and the pattern holds in chimpanzees too
**Work ID:** W4414713988 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal chain asserted for gender gap** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "a reminder that the gender gap in measured risk appetite feeds directly into who ends up in charge"
- **Abstract says:** "Risk preference also correlated positively with average income in humans and was generally higher in males than females in both species."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports two separate correlations (risk preference with leadership; sex differences in risk preference). It does not test or claim that the sex difference in risk preference causes or mediates who attains leadership.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as 'men scored higher on risk preference on average, and risk preference was associated with leadership — though the study does not test whether one explains the other'."

**Issue B. Speculation presented as a finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "while also showing that human organizations weaken the link — presumably through formal selection, training and culture"
- **Abstract says:** "A stronger connection between leadership and risk-taking in chimpanzees may be attributable to cultural mechanisms for the transmission of leadership in human societies."
- **Problem:** The abstract offers this as a possible interpretation ('may be attributable'); the digest presents it as something the study showed.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write 'the authors suggest the weaker human link may reflect cultural mechanisms for transmitting leadership'."

### Study 10: Your energy peaks at noon — and what you do before lunch decides how hard you crash
**Work ID:** W7202007719 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Dropped qualifier on afternoon effort** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "high afternoon effort predicted a steeper evening decline"
- **Abstract says:** "During the resource retraction phase, afternoon effort predicted a steeper evening decline, although this association did not persist once absorption was included."
- **Problem:** The abstract explicitly reports that this association did not hold once absorption was in the model. Omitting that makes a non-robust result look solid, and the 'Why it matters' and manager angle build on it.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add the qualifier: 'high afternoon effort predicted a steeper evening decline, though this association disappeared once absorption was accounted for'."

**Issue B. Direction added to the absorption interaction** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "deeply absorbed, low-effort work was followed by gentler evening declines"
- **Abstract says:** "absorption differentiated evening declines primarily when effort was low, whereas differences were smaller under high effort"
- **Problem:** The abstract says absorption differentiated evening trajectories under low effort but does not state the direction (gentler vs steeper).
- **Suggested fix:** "Write 'absorption made the biggest difference to evening declines when effort was low' without specifying the direction, or verify from the full text."

**Issue C. Causal language in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "what you do before lunch decides how hard you crash"
- **Abstract says:** "Using experience sampling data from 106 employees over 10 workdays … we applied piecewise growth curve modeling to analyze within-person variations in energy trajectories."
- **Problem:** These are within-person associations from observational experience sampling with self-reported effort, sleep and energy; 'decides' asserts causation. The digest's caveats note this, but the headline and 'Why it matters' still read causally.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use 'is associated with how hard you crash' or 'tracks how hard you crash'."

### Study 11: Career stress doesn't push students to plan — it pushes them to avoid
**Work ID:** W7204791998 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal language for correlational chain** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Approach behavior led to choice progress, which in turn reduced later stress."
- **Abstract says:** "Avoidance- and approach-oriented career behaviors related negatively and positively to choice progress, respectively, which in turn was negatively related to subsequent decisional stress."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports associations across waves, not causal effects; "led to" and "reduced" imply demonstrated causation.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "Approach behavior was associated with choice progress, which in turn was linked to lower later stress.""

**Issue B. Moderator tests attributed to both studies** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Core self-evaluation and peer career support did not buffer the stress–avoidance link"
- **Abstract says:** "Study 2 ( N = 176) replicated these findings. Furthermore, we explored the moderating role of core self-evaluation (CSE) and peer career support."
- **Problem:** The moderation analyses were conducted only in Study 2, but the digest reads as if they span both studies.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add "In Study 2…" before the moderator sentence."

**Issue C. "Dissolves the stress" overstates** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "while concrete progress is what actually dissolves the stress"
- **Abstract says:** "choice progress, which in turn was negatively related to subsequent decisional stress"
- **Problem:** A negative association is presented as a demonstrated remedy.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "progress is what tracks with lower stress later on.""

### Study 16: When your partner or child is job hunting, your own work suffers
**Work ID:** W7211937530 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline states an unconditional effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "When your partner or child is job hunting, your own work suffers"
- **Abstract says:** "Findings from both studies provide partial support for the moderation of job search interdependence in our model, as the pattern of findings shows an asymmetry by the type of job‐seeking family member (i.e., child versus spouse). In Study 2, the hypothesized three‐way interaction with work identity was not supported."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only partial, conditional support (depending on interdependence, family member type and — in exploratory analysis — work identity); the headline presents a universal spillover effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "Hedge the headline, e.g., "A family member's job hunt can spill into your own work — for some helpers.""

**Issue B. Main-effect link asserted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Active job search by a family member was linked to greater family-to-work conflict for the non-searcher"
- **Abstract says:** "we hypothesize that job search interdependence moderates the relationship between family member active job search behavior and non‐job‐seeker family–work conflict"
- **Problem:** The abstract frames this as a hypothesized, moderated relationship with only partial support, not an established overall association.
- **Suggested fix:** "Lead with the conditional framing: "Whether a family member's job search raised family–work conflict depended on how interdependent the search was.""

### Study 19: Followers rated a female leader's vision as more competent than the same vision from a man
**Work ID:** W7212844454 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Hypotheses reported as findings** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Women who led with vision were perceived as more competent than men doing the same, consistent with expectancy violation rather than backlash; those competence perceptions predicted follower identification with the leader and, in turn, support for organizational change. The pattern was strongest among male followers."
- **Abstract says:** "we argue that … the effect of visionary leadership on perceptions of competence is more strongly positive than when male leaders do so. We argue that these competence perceptions are then positively related to follower identification … we propose that these effects are particularly pronounced among male followers. We test our hypotheses using an experimental vignette study and a three‐wave leader‐follower field study."
- **Problem:** The abstract only states what the authors argue and propose, and that hypotheses were tested; it never reports whether they were supported. The digest converts the theoretical model into confirmed results.
- **Suggested fix:** "State that the paper predicts these effects and tests them in two studies, and note that the abstract does not report the results — label the specifics "unverifiable from abstract only.""

**Issue B. Headline asserts an unreported result** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Followers rated a female leader's vision as more competent than the same vision from a man"
- **Abstract says:** "Our findings advance understanding of how gender shapes the effects of visionary leadership on follower support for change."
- **Problem:** The headline presents a specific effect size/direction that the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe the headline around the competing predictions tested (backlash vs. expectancy violation) pending the full text."

**Issue C. Caveats omit the results gap** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "abstract does not report sample sizes or the field study's country and occupation"
- **Abstract says:** "We test our hypotheses using an experimental vignette study and a three‐wave leader‐follower field study."
- **Problem:** The caveat flags missing sample details but not the more important fact that the abstract reports no findings at all.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that the abstract does not report results, so all reported effects are unverifiable from the abstract."

### Study 1: Seven years inside a company that went remote: psychological safety got stronger, not weaker
**Work ID:** W7203941475 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Illustrative details not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the researcher found that remote interaction patterns — glimpses of homes, kids, pets, flexible scheduling conversations — prompted more personal self-disclosure among colleagues"
- **Abstract says:** "I find that remote work supports interactional patterns that facilitate self-disclosure among group members."
- **Problem:** The specific examples of what the interaction patterns were (homes, kids, pets, scheduling talk) are the digest's invention; the abstract describes the patterns only in general terms. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Either drop the concrete examples or label them as illustration, e.g. 'everyday virtual interaction patterns (the paper's examples of these are not visible in the abstract) prompted more personal self-disclosure'."

### Study 3: The "work companion": the coworking-space relationship that isn't friendship and isn't colleagues
**Work ID:** W7202178589 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Sample composition claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "coworking-space members are self-selected and skew toward freelancers and independent workers, so findings may not transfer to employees of large firms"
- **Abstract says:** "workers ... work alongside others with different employers, occupations, and backgrounds ... interview data collected from 80 workers in 21 coworking spaces"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not describe the occupational mix of the sample; it actually refers to members having 'different employers', so the freelancer skew is not verifiable from the abstract alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to 'coworking-space members are self-selected, and the abstract does not report their mix of employment types, so transfer to employees of large firms is untested'."

### Study 5: Return-to-office works better when teammates, not bosses, provide the support
**Work ID:** W7204438985 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Withdrawal finding restated as a level change** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "found its link to individual performance was not significant immediately after the return but became positive six months later, while work withdrawal behaviors declined over the same period"
- **Abstract says:** "Results revealed that the non-significant impact of job reattachment on performance changed to a positive significant impact, while the impact of work withdrawal reduced over a six-month duration."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that the (reattachment-related) impact on/of work withdrawal weakened over time — a change in a relationship, not evidence that employees' withdrawal behaviour itself fell. The digest converts this into an absolute decline in withdrawal, which readers could take as 'returning to the office reduces withdrawal'.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as 'while the reattachment–withdrawal association weakened over the six months' rather than claiming withdrawal behaviours declined."

**Issue B. Supervisory dependence moderation narrowed** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Reliance on a dependable supervisor boosted the reattachment–engagement link only in the first phase"
- **Abstract says:** "supervisory dependence was found to be a significant moderator in the reattachment–engagement link, and it demonstrated a significant moderated-mediation effect in phase 1 but became non-significant in phase 2"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports supervisory dependence as a significant moderator of the reattachment–engagement link generally; it was the moderated-mediation effect that was confined to phase 1. The digest attaches the phase-1-only qualifier to the wrong effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say that supervisory dependence moderated the reattachment–engagement link, but its downstream (moderated-mediation) effect held only in the first phase."

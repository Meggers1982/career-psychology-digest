### 1. Why "we grew sales" beats "we cut costs" — even when the dollars are identical

**Journal:** *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* | **Published:** 2026-09-30
**Work ID:** W7214937827 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.obhdp.2026.104525

**The study:** Across nine experiments (N=6,045), four supplemental studies and archival analysis of roughly 13 million financial news articles, researchers tested how people judge a $1 million revenue increase versus a $1 million expense cut — mathematically identical effects on profit. The revenue version consistently produced rosier judgments of a company's profitability, financial strength and trajectory; managers who proposed revenue-growth ideas were rated more competent, consultants who devised them were more likely to be rehired, and companies looked more attractive to investors. Part of the explanation is that revenue strategies simply seem more innovative. In the archival data, firms reporting revenue increases rather than expense decreases subsequently climbed in Fortune's "World's Most Admired Companies" rankings.

**Why it matters:** How a financial result is framed — top line versus cost line — changes how competent its author looks and how much capital follows, independent of the actual economics.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you run a budget or pitch a business case, notice that the cost-savings proposal you're proud of may be systematically undervalued relative to an equivalent growth proposal; the experiments suggest reframing savings in terms of what they enable, and that review committees may want to compare proposals on net profit impact rather than narrative. Also worth watching internally: whether your firm's promotion and bonus patterns quietly reward growth stories over efficiency work.
- **For general readers:** The finding helps explain why cost-cutting jobs — procurement, operations, back-office efficiency — carry less prestige than sales and growth roles, and why "we're expanding" sounds like good news while "we're economizing" sounds like trouble, even when the bank balance is the same.

**Caveats:** Most evidence comes from controlled experiments with evaluators making hypothetical judgments rather than real investors committing money; the archival Fortune analysis is correlational and cannot rule out that revenue-growing firms differ in other ways. Sample composition (likely heavy use of online panels) is not specified in the abstract.

### 2. Field experiment in Indian markets: identical stalls drew 41% more customers when a woman was selling

**Journal:** *Organization Science* | **Published:** 2026-08-18
**Work ID:** W7203695857 | **DOI:** 10.1287/orsc.2024.18859

**The study:** Researchers set up matched, newly created microenterprises in two face-to-face markets in India, staffing them with trained confederate sellers who ran identical businesses and followed the same customer-engagement protocols — isolating seller gender as the variable. Female sellers attracted 41% more customers and made significantly higher sales than men, holding business characteristics and selling behavior constant. Customers were also more likely to chat with women about topics unrelated to the sale and more likely to try to bargain with them.

**Why it matters:** It inverts the usual story about women's lower entrepreneurial earnings: in customer-facing selling, approachability can be an economic advantage, meaning the well-documented gender earnings gap among microentrepreneurs likely stems from structural constraints rather than the sales interaction itself.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For anyone managing customer-facing staff or retail floors, this is evidence that who customers choose to approach is a measurable business variable, not noise — worth tracking approach rates and conversation length alongside conversion. The flip side managers should watch: women also faced more bargaining attempts and more off-topic conversation, which is unpaid interactional labor that can erode margins and time.
- **For general readers:** The study shows how much of commercial life turns on a split-second judgment about whether a stranger looks warm and approachable — and that the same stereotype that invites more customers also invites more haggling.

**Caveats:** Confederate sellers running de novo stalls are not established business owners, so results may not transfer to longer-term customer relationships; two markets in one country; the design isolates customer approach and deliberately holds constant the structural factors (capital, networks, mobility) that drive real earnings gaps.

### 3. Feeling grateful made people pay more for green products — and blunted the money-grab reflex after climate news

**Journal:** *Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-21
**Work ID:** W7213965712 | **DOI:** 10.1111/apps.70144

**The study:** Three preregistered studies with 2,100 participants tested whether gratitude shifts economic choices. A correlational multisource study (N=850) linked dispositional gratitude to sustainable economic preferences and green investment intentions, partly through lower materialism and greater nature connectedness. An experiment (N=650) found a gratitude induction — compared with neutral and positive-affect controls — raised willingness to pay for eco-labeled products and green investment allocation, mediated by reduced momentary desire for money. A third 2×2 experiment (N=600) showed climate change information actually increased money desire, but gratitude buffered that effect.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the emotional framing around sustainability messaging matters: alarming climate information alone may push people toward grasping for money, while a brief gratitude prompt may counteract it.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Communications and ESG teams running internal sustainability pushes could test gratitude framing against threat framing — but note this is lab-style choice behavior, not procurement or budget decisions, so treat it as a hypothesis worth piloting rather than a proven technique.
- **For general readers:** The counterintuitive bit for general readers: being told about climate change made people want money more, not less — a reminder that fear-based messaging can backfire on the very behavior it targets.

**Caveats:** Outcomes are stated intentions and hypothetical allocations, not real purchases or investments; mediation in Study 1 is cross-sectional; effects are momentary and short-horizon, with no evidence they persist; sample details and recruitment source are not specified in the abstract.

### 4. When new women join the board, firms are more likely to build abroad than buy abroad

**Journal:** *The Journal of Applied Behavioral Science* | **Published:** 2026-08-17
**Work ID:** W7203630460 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00218863261478348

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 2,009 firm-year observations from Taiwanese manufacturing companies, linking the appointment of new female directors to how those firms entered foreign markets — building operations from scratch (greenfield) versus acquiring an existing company (M&A). Firms that had recently appointed more female directors leaned toward greenfield entry. The tilt was stronger when a woman chaired the board, and weaker when incumbent directors already had substantial international experience.

**Why it matters:** Board composition isn't just a governance or optics issue — it appears to show up in one of the biggest financial decisions a company makes, how it expands across borders. It also suggests that who already sits in the room determines whether a new appointee's perspective actually changes the outcome.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For executives and board members: a new director's influence is conditional on context, not automatic. If you're adding directors to broaden decision-making, this study hints that an ally in the chair's seat amplifies their voice while an experienced incumbent bloc can absorb it — worth watching in your own committee dynamics and succession planning.
- **For general readers:** For general readers: a window into how corporate decisions that look purely financial — build a factory or buy a competitor — can trace back to who was recently invited onto the board and whether anyone at the table backed them up.

**Caveats:** Archival, correlational data — no causal test, and the study cannot rule out that firms already planning greenfield entry appoint different directors. Single country (Taiwan) and single sector (manufacturing). The psychological mechanisms invoked (risk propensity, the chair supporting new female directors in discussion) are inferred from outcomes rather than directly observed.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Three of the four studies show that decisions presented as hard economics are actually decided at the perception layer: identical dollars get judged differently depending on whether they arrive as revenue or savings ("Why 'we grew sales' beats 'we cut costs'", W7214937827), identical market stalls get 41% more customers depending on who is standing behind them ("Field experiment in Indian markets", W7203695857), and identical foreign-expansion options get chosen differently depending on who recently joined the board and whether the chair backs them ("When new women join the board", W7203630460). The gratitude study rounds it out from the emotional side, finding that climate information increased people's desire for money while a brief gratitude prompt blunted it ("Feeling grateful made people pay more for green products", W7213965712) — the same dollars, re-weighted by mood.

**Why pitch this now:** Companies are deep into an efficiency cycle — AI-justified headcount reduction, flattened middle management, cost programs sold to boards as discipline — at exactly the moment when the evidence says efficiency work is systematically undercredited relative to growth narratives, and when board-composition changes are being rolled back as cosmetic.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the "perception tax" inside supposedly numerical business decisions — who gets credited for a dollar, who gets approached by a customer, whose voice in the boardroom actually moves an outcome — written for managers and ambitious professionals who are being evaluated by exactly these distorted yardsticks, with practical decision-hygiene fixes (comparing proposals on net profit impact, tracking approach rates, noting whether a new appointee has an ally in the room).

**Potential headlines:**
- The Same Dollar Is Worth More If You Call It Growth
- Cost-Cutters Save the Company and Lose the Promotion
- Your Next Raise May Depend on Which Side of the Ledger You Work On

**Potential outlets:**
- **Harvard Business Review** — the net-profit-impact comparison rule and the board-chair-as-amplifier finding are exactly the kind of evidence-backed decision-hygiene prescriptions its management readership acts on.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — strong fit for the governance half, linking new-director influence to a concrete capital-allocation choice (greenfield versus acquisition) rather than to diversity optics.
- **The Atlantic** — the cultural read on why expansion sounds like health and thrift sounds like decline, and what that does to the status of back-office and operations careers.
- **Business Insider** — its workplace desk tracks who gets promoted during efficiency drives, and the revenue-versus-savings credit gap is a direct, reportable angle on current cost-cutting rounds.

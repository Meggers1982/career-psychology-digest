### 1. It's not which evening activity you choose — it's the mix

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-28
**Work ID:** W7169828568 | **DOI:** 10.1037/apl0001424

**The study:** Researchers took a person-centered approach to after-work recovery, using daily diary data (the replication sample comprised 321 employees providing 1,780 daily observations) to see whether evening activities cluster into recognizable patterns rather than acting one at a time. Distinct profiles of evening activity combinations emerged and largely replicated across two independent samples. Those profiles were linked to different recovery experiences (such as detachment and relaxation), well-being, and next-day work outcomes, and were predicted by what had happened at work that day.

**Why it matters:** Advice to 'exercise' or 'socialize' after work treats activities in isolation; this evidence suggests the shape of a whole evening — the combination of activities — is what predicts how recovered and effective you are the next morning.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers who push single wellness levers (a gym benefit, a no-email-after-6 rule) may be missing the point: ask whether workloads leave employees able to assemble a whole restorative evening, and watch how demanding days push people into less restorative evening patterns.
- **For general readers:** For readers, a useful reframe of the nightly wind-down: the question is less 'is screen time bad?' and more 'what does my typical evening add up to, and do I wake up feeling restored?'

**Caveats:** Diary self-reports of activities, recovery and outcomes come from the same person (common-method concerns); profiles are descriptive clusters, not experimentally manipulated, so causal claims about next-day outcomes are limited; the published abstract is truncated and does not fully describe the first sample or the occupations involved.

### 2. When the boss talks about their own good deeds, half the team is inspired and half is repelled

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-03
**Work ID:** W7167868566 | **DOI:** 10.1037/apl0001416

**The study:** Across a field study of 254 supervisor–employee dyads and an experiment with 233 employees, researchers examined what happens when supervisors publicly share their own involvement in morally positive events — volunteering, helping, doing the right thing. Self-focused moral talk raised employees' feelings of moral elevation and, through that, their citizenship behavior. But for supervisors who rarely highlighted other people's good deeds, the same self-focused talk instead produced employee disgust and less citizenship behavior.

**Why it matters:** Leaders are routinely coached to 'model values' out loud, but this evidence shows the same message reads as inspiring or as holier-than-thou virtue signaling depending on whether the leader also credits others.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** A practical test for leaders: audit your ratio of 'what I did' to 'what my people did' when describing the team's good works — the study suggests sharing the spotlight is what keeps moral storytelling from curdling into self-promotion.
- **For general readers:** It names a familiar workplace irritation — the colleague or boss whose good deeds always come with an announcement — and explains why the reaction is visceral disgust rather than mild annoyance.

**Caveats:** The field portion is cross-sectional dyadic survey data, so causality is inferred rather than established; the supporting experiment is relatively small (N=233 across conditions) and uses a controlled scenario; employee emotions and citizenship ratings rely partly on self-report.

### 3. Mindfulness training may work by teaching people to build resources, not just protect them

**Journal:** *Organization Science* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7204869548 | **DOI:** 10.1287/orsc.2022.17009

**The study:** Using longitudinal qualitative data from 98 nonprofit employees going through mindfulness training, researchers built a theory of 'mindful resourcing': rather than mindfulness being a limited inner reserve that gets depleted, they describe it as a practice people use to create and enact new resources at work. They document 'resourcing spirals' in which employees string together small resource-creating moves and, with practice, extend them into longer and more consequential sequences.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the dominant 'resources are scarce, so conserve them' logic behind much burnout advice, suggesting training can expand what employees have to work with rather than only slowing the drain.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For managers evaluating mindfulness or resilience programs: the payoff this research points to is skill in generating support, time and attention — so measure whether participants actually mobilize new resources, not just whether they feel calmer.
- **For general readers:** A different way to think about feeling stretched thin: some of what feels like a fixed shortage of time and energy may be a skill gap in assembling help, rather than a hard limit.

**Caveats:** Qualitative theory-building in a single training context with nonprofit employees; no control group, no quantitative outcomes, and no test of whether resourcing spirals improve performance or reduce burnout; funded by a California State University, Northridge RSCA Award.

### 4. The causes CEOs shout about loudest are the ones their firms are least likely to act on

**Journal:** *Organization Science* | **Published:** 2026-09-04
**Work ID:** W7208750164 | **DOI:** 10.1287/orsc.2026.23114

**The study:** This three-essay Organization Science paper examines why CEOs take public stances on sociopolitical issues, whether those stances are followed by real firm initiatives, and how they ripple outward. Alongside political ideology, it introduces CEO narcissism as a driver of attention-seeking public activism. The central pattern across the essays: the issues most likely to draw attention to a CEO are the least likely to be matched by firm follow-through — though they still shift public and political debate.

**Why it matters:** It gives employees, investors and boards a sharper way to read executive statements on social issues: attention-grabbing stances and substantive corporate commitments appear to be partly decoupled.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For leaders and comms teams: before putting out a public stance, ask whether the issue is one the firm can actually resource — the research suggests the most visible topics are the hardest to back with action, which is exactly where credibility is lost.
- **For general readers:** For anyone who has watched a CEO's social-issue statement land in their inbox: the findings suggest the personality of the person at the top, not just company values, shapes what gets said — and that saying it may still move public debate even when nothing changes internally.

**Caveats:** The published abstract is a dissertation summary and does not specify samples, data sources or analytic methods for the three essays, so the strength of evidence cannot be judged from it; CEO narcissism is typically measured indirectly through archival proxies; funded by the French National Research Agency (ANR-18-EURE-0005 / EUR DATA EFM).

### 5. Why the best-connected person in the room is the hardest to trust

**Journal:** *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* | **Published:** 2026-09-06
**Work ID:** W7210270541 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.obhdp.2026.104523

**The study:** Researchers studied referral brokerage — people who connect two otherwise unconnected parties — using one field study plus two experiments. They found a paradox: networks rich in "structural holes" put brokers in the best position to spot valuable matches, but leave recipients with fewer shared contacts to vouch for the broker, eroding the trust needed to accept the referral. Brokers who engaged in "network self-promotion" — explicitly signaling the quality of their network and their matchmaking skill — closed the gap, but asymmetrically: self-promotion substituted for missing reputational cues about ability, amplified integrity-based trust when shared ties already existed, and did nothing for benevolence-based trust.

**Why it matters:** Referrals and introductions are how a huge share of jobs, clients and deals get made, and this shows the people best positioned to make them often face the steepest credibility barrier.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you broker introductions — recruiters, business developers, senior managers connecting teams — the research suggests being explicit about why you are well placed to make the match, especially when you and the recipient share no mutual contacts. Worth testing in your own outreach rather than assuming a bare introduction carries weight.
- **For general readers:** For anyone receiving an unsolicited introduction, the findings explain the instinctive hesitation: without mutual contacts, you are being asked to trust a stranger's judgment about another stranger. Knowing which part of trust is missing — competence versus good intentions — helps you ask the right follow-up questions.

**Caveats:** Two of the three studies are experiments, likely with online participants, and the paper does not describe the field study sample size or setting in the abstract; referral acceptance in experiments may not match real stakes.

### 6. Leaders take more risks — and the pattern holds in chimpanzees too

**Journal:** *The Leadership Quarterly* | **Published:** 2026-09-07
**Work ID:** W4414713988 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.leaqua.2026.101992

**The study:** Researchers combined data from the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP), a large nationally representative household survey, with observer reports of chimpanzees at two African sanctuaries. In both species, higher risk preference was associated with leadership status (humans) and hierarchical rank (chimpanzees). The association was considerably stronger in chimpanzees than in humans; risk preference also tracked higher average income among humans and was higher in males in both species.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the link between appetite for risk and rising to the top is not purely a modern corporate artifact, while also showing that human organizations weaken the link — presumably through formal selection, training and culture.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If risk appetite quietly predicts who gets promoted, promotion systems may be selecting on a trait that is useful in some roles and dangerous in others. Managers could ask whether their own leadership pipeline rewards boldness independently of judgment and results.
- **For general readers:** For readers wondering why cautious, competent colleagues get passed over, this offers an uncomfortable partial explanation — and a reminder that the gender gap in measured risk appetite feeds directly into who ends up in charge.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional and correlational — risk preference may follow from holding power rather than cause it. Human data from a single country (Germany); chimpanzee data rely on observer ratings at two sanctuaries. The cross-species comparison is suggestive, not evidence of a shared mechanism.

### 7. Companies craft 'purpose' statements all wrong — the useful part is the discomfort

**Journal:** *Academy of Management Journal* | **Published:** 2026-09-08
**Work ID:** W7172567426 | **DOI:** 10.5465/amj.2024.0294

**The study:** An ethnographic study followed facilitated purpose-creation processes inside top management teams at companies across several industries, analyzing both successful and failed attempts. The researchers found that emotional dynamics — particularly negative emotions, and the coexistence of conflicting positive and negative emotions — drove whether executives genuinely committed to the purpose they wrote. Processes that deliberately maintained discomfort and prevented premature closure, and that managed timing, spacing and physical materials, produced stronger commitment.

**Why it matters:** Most purpose and values exercises are designed to feel good and reach consensus fast, which this research suggests is exactly why they produce statements nobody believes.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you run strategy offsites or values workshops, the finding argues for resisting the urge to resolve tension on day one: sitting with unresolved conflict appears to be what converts a slogan into commitment. A testable change to how your next leadership retreat is structured.
- **For general readers:** Explains the familiar experience of a corporate mission statement that nobody can recall — the discomfort was engineered out of the process that created it.

**Caveats:** Qualitative ethnography across a small set of companies; no quantitative test, no measure of whether employees downstream ever bought in, and findings depend on the researchers' interpretation of facilitated sessions.

### 8. In family firms, the younger sibling joins first — and leaves soonest

**Journal:** *Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214180006 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10422587261485870

**The study:** Using Swedish registry-type data and family-stratified hazard models, researchers tracked next-generation career choices in family businesses: joining, holding a managerial post, and leaving. Later-born children were more likely than their older siblings to join the family firm as their first career move. But once inside, later-borns were less likely to reach managerial positions and more likely to exit altogether.

**Why it matters:** Succession planning in family businesses often assumes willingness to join signals commitment, when this suggests the early joiners may be the ones least likely to end up running the place.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Family firm owners and advisors should separate who shows up first from who should lead: this evidence indicates birth order shapes entry and exit patterns in ways that can quietly misallocate the next generation. Worth examining whether managerial roles are being reserved for elder siblings regardless of fit.
- **For general readers:** A window into how family dynamics most of us recognize — the eldest expected to prove themselves outside, the youngest drawn back home — translate into real career trajectories and real money.

**Caveats:** Single country (Sweden), where family, inheritance and labor market norms may be distinctive; observational data, so birth order effects are associational and mechanism tests are indirect; culture-specific primogeniture norms may not generalize.

### 9. 'Happiness hero' and 'chief storyteller': what weird job titles actually cost you

**Journal:** *Academy of Management Review* | **Published:** 2026-09-15
**Work ID:** W7213226017 | **DOI:** 10.5465/amr.2024.0311

**The study:** This is a theory paper taking a strategic HR perspective on the rise of unconventional job titles. It argues organizations use such titles to signal two things — that new tasks have been bundled into a genuinely novel role, and that a conventional role has been rebranded to convey the company's symbolic identity — and sorts titles into four types: conventional, elastic, semantic and transfigured. It then specifies two costs: "legibility costs" when attracting candidates who cannot tell what the job is, and "verification costs" at selection when neither side can benchmark the role.

**Why it matters:** Quirky titles are treated as free branding, but this frames them as a signal with real downstream costs in applicant pools and in employees' ability to prove their experience later.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Before signing off on a 'vibe manager' req, managers can ask which of the two signals the title is actually sending, and whether the recruiting pipeline can absorb the loss of searchability and comparability.
- **For general readers:** For job seekers: an unconventional title can make your next move harder, because recruiters outside the company cannot decode what you did — a practical reason to negotiate for a legible title or a legible internal equivalent.

**Caveats:** Purely conceptual — no new data, no empirical test of whether unconventional titles actually reduce applicant quality or hurt later careers. Included for the framework rather than for evidence.

### 10. Your energy peaks at noon — and what you do before lunch decides how hard you crash

**Journal:** *Journal of Business and Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-08-28
**Work ID:** W7202007719 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10869-026-10144-w

**The study:** Researchers used experience sampling with 106 employees who reported four times a day across 10 workdays (2,552 observations), then modeled daily energy as a piecewise growth curve. Energy followed an inverted-U, rising to a midday peak and declining afterward. Good sleep raised the starting level of energy but flattened the morning climb; high effort at noon predicted a steeper afternoon drop, and high afternoon effort predicted a steeper evening decline. Absorption in work mattered mainly when effort was low — deeply absorbed, low-effort work was followed by gentler evening declines.

**Why it matters:** It reframes daily energy as a managed resource with distinct phases rather than a fixed trait, suggesting that when demanding work is scheduled matters as much as how much of it there is.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers could test scheduling cognitively heavy tasks around the natural midday peak and protecting the late afternoon, and treat chronic steep evening crashes in a team as a scheduling and workload signal rather than a motivation problem. This is one modest sample over two weeks, so treat it as a hypothesis to try with your own team's rhythms.
- **For general readers:** It gives a concrete explanation for why some evenings feel wrecked and others don't: sleep sets your starting tank, but heavy effort in the second half of the day is what makes the drop steep — and losing yourself in work isn't automatically restorative once effort is high.

**Caveats:** Self-reported energy, effort and sleep from the same people (common-method concerns); 106 employees over 10 workdays is a short horizon; associations are within-person correlations, not experimental manipulations of effort or sleep.

### 11. Career stress doesn't push students to plan — it pushes them to avoid

**Journal:** *Journal of Career Development* | **Published:** 2026-08-30
**Work ID:** W7204791998 | **DOI:** 10.1177/08948453261477660

**The study:** Two multi-wave studies of master's students (N=126 and N=176) tracked career decision-making stress alongside two kinds of self-regulatory behavior: approach-oriented steps (researching options, making progress) and avoidance-oriented steps (distracting oneself, putting the decision off). In both studies, decisional stress predicted more avoidance but was unrelated to approach behavior. Core self-evaluation and peer career support did not buffer the stress–avoidance link, but each had direct effects: higher core self-evaluation meant less avoidance, and peer support meant more approach behavior. Approach behavior led to choice progress, which in turn reduced later stress.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that pressure alone does not motivate career decisions — it mostly fuels procrastination — while concrete progress is what actually dissolves the stress.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For managers and mentors advising people on career moves, and for early-career professionals themselves: piling on urgency is unlikely to produce action, whereas structured peer conversations and small completed steps appear to break the loop. Findings come from master's students, so the mechanism is suggestive for working adults rather than demonstrated.
- **For general readers:** Anyone who has doom-scrolled instead of updating a résumé will recognize the pattern: career anxiety and career action are not the same engine, and the only reliable exit found here was visible progress, which then lowered the stress.

**Caveats:** Student samples (master's students) rather than working adults; self-report measures throughout; modest sample sizes; multi-wave but correlational, so causal direction is inferred rather than tested.

### 12. Parents and teachers build career confidence; friends spark career curiosity

**Journal:** *Journal of Career Assessment* | **Published:** 2026-08-31
**Work ID:** W7204791763 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10690727261485959

**The study:** Researchers followed 1,952 Chinese adolescents (average age 15.5) across three annual waves of senior high school and used network analysis to map how the dimensions of career adaptability connect to each other and to perceived career-efficacy contributions from parents, teachers and peers. Curiosity and confidence were consistently the most central nodes in the network over time. Support from parents and teachers was most strongly tied to career confidence, while peer support was most closely tied to career curiosity.

**Why it matters:** It indicates that different people in a young person's life feed different parts of career readiness, so generic 'support' advice misses which lever each source actually pulls.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For managers running internships, apprenticeships or early-career programs, the finding hints that authority figures and peers are not interchangeable mentors: supervisors may be better placed to build confidence, while peer cohorts drive exploration and curiosity. Test whether your program design uses both.
- **For general readers:** For parents and teachers wondering how to help a teenager figure out a career: the evidence suggests your role is less about supplying options than about building belief they can handle a choice — friends do more of the exploring.

**Caveats:** Single country (China) and a single school-age cohort; all measures self-reported by adolescents; network associations are correlational even across three waves; findings may not transfer to adult workplaces.

### 13. Stuck at the same grade for years? That's where quiet quitting starts

**Journal:** *Career Development International* | **Published:** 2026-08-31
**Work ID:** W7204821646 | **DOI:** 10.1108/cdi-11-2025-0690

**The study:** A time-lagged survey of faculty members at public and private higher-education institutions, sampled through multistage clustering, tested whether a hierarchical career plateau — feeling permanently stuck in one's current position — predicts quiet quitting. Using PLS-SEM, the researchers found a positive link, mediated in parallel by a drop in felt obligation toward the employer and a loss of meaningfulness in the work.

**Why it matters:** It locates quiet quitting in a structural cause employers control — blocked promotion ladders — rather than in generational attitude, and points to two specific psychological casualties: reciprocity and meaning.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If disengagement is spreading in a flat or slow-promoting organization, the practical test is whether plateaued staff still feel the work means something and still feel they owe the employer effort; lateral moves, scope expansion and visible recognition may matter more than another engagement survey.
- **For general readers:** For readers plateaued in their own jobs, the study names what happens next: quiet withdrawal usually begins when the sense of mutual obligation and meaning erodes, not when the job gets harder.

**Caveats:** Single occupation (academic faculty) in one country context; self-report survey with predictor and outcomes from the same source; time-lagged rather than experimental, so causation is inferred; mediation modeling of this type is correlational evidence.

### 14. Don't start your day replaying yesterday's mistakes

**Journal:** *Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-02
**Work ID:** W7207539887 | **DOI:** 10.1111/apps.70137

**The study:** Researchers ran a 10-day randomized experience-sampling field experiment with 102 full-time employees. Each morning, workers were randomly assigned to reflect on the previous day's successes, their errors, or neutral work events, and then reported their behavior through the day. Reflecting on successes raised exploration behavior (trying new approaches, seeking new information) by increasing attentiveness, while reflecting on errors reduced exploration via lower attentiveness and higher shame — an effect that was worse for employees driven to prove their competence to others.

**Why it matters:** The popular advice to "learn from your mistakes" through daily reflection may backfire: error-focused reflection appears to shut down the very experimentation that learning depends on.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers running daily stand-ups, after-action reviews or reflective journaling should test whether a success-first framing produces more experimentation than an error-first one, and be aware that competitive, status-conscious employees may react worst to being asked to dwell on errors.
- **For general readers:** For anyone who ends or starts the day mentally reviewing what went wrong: this randomized experiment suggests that habit can produce shame and narrowed attention rather than insight, and that deliberately recalling what went right may leave you more curious the next day.

**Caveats:** Single study with 102 employees over 10 workdays; outcomes are self-reported daily behavior; short time horizon means long-term learning effects are unknown; sample and national context not fully specified in the abstract.

### 15. A leadership program measurably raised women's ambition to run for office

**Journal:** *Journal of Vocational Behavior* | **Published:** 2026-09-04
**Work ID:** W7208741244 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jvb.2026.104283

**The study:** Researchers evaluated a women-only political leadership development program — covering the political process, access to serving politicians and officials, and leadership skill-building — using matched pre- and post-intervention data from 73 participants. Participation increased women's political ambition, strengthened political self-efficacy and lowered perceived gender-, experience- and guidance-related barriers. Only increased political self-efficacy and reduced perceptions of gender barriers actually explained the rise in ambition, and the program broke down gender barriers most for women with nontraditional gender role attitudes.

**Why it matters:** It offers a tested template for pipeline programs in any male-dominated field: confidence in one's own capability and the belief that gender barriers are surmountable are the levers that move ambition, not information or networking alone.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If your organization runs a women's leadership or sponsorship program, audit whether it actually builds domain-specific self-efficacy and directly addresses gender barriers, rather than simply offering generic skills training and networking — those were the two mechanisms that mattered here.
- **For general readers:** Ambition isn't a fixed trait. This study suggests that what keeps capable women out of male-dominated careers is partly a believable sense that the path is navigable — and a few months of structured exposure can shift it.

**Caveats:** Small sample (n=73 matched pre/post) and no control group, so change can't be cleanly attributed to the program; self-reported ambition rather than actual candidacy; self-selected participants who already aspired to office; single program in one country.

### 16. When your partner or child is job hunting, your own work suffers

**Journal:** *Journal of Organizational Behavior* | **Published:** 2026-09-08
**Work ID:** W7211937530 | **DOI:** 10.1002/job.70128

**The study:** Two studies examined the overlooked spillover from a family member's job search onto the non-searcher. Study 1 was a two-wave dyadic field study of 178 undergraduate job seekers paired with their employed parents; Study 2 was a scenario-based experiment with 483 participants imagining a job-seeking spouse. Active job search by a family member was linked to greater family-to-work conflict for the non-searcher, with the pattern depending on how interdependent the search was and differing between child and spouse searchers; the effect appeared mainly among non-searchers with strong work identity.

**Why it matters:** Job searching is usually treated as an individual project, but it draws down a household's shared time and emotional resources — and the person not searching can end up distracted or depleted at their own job.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers seeing unexplained dips in focus should consider household disruption, including a partner's or adult child's job hunt, as a legitimate (and temporary) source of work-family conflict, and employees supporting a searcher should set boundaries on how much of the search they absorb.
- **For general readers:** For families: helping a graduating child or unemployed spouse find work is real labor. Explicitly dividing up who does what — rather than drifting into shared ownership of someone else's search — may protect the helper's own job.

**Caveats:** Study 1's job seekers were undergraduates and their parents, limiting generalizability; Study 2 used hypothetical scenarios rather than real job searches; several hypotheses received only partial support and the three-way interaction was not supported, with the key finding emerging from exploratory follow-up analyses; self-report measures.

### 17. Matching a coach to the person beats matching the method to the goal

**Journal:** *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7212149146 | **DOI:** 10.1111/joop.70155

**The study:** Two experimental vignette studies tested how coaching approach should be chosen: Study 1 asked 198 professional coaches to rate the fit between solution-focused coaching (SFC) and cognitive-behavioral coaching (CBC) and different goals; Study 2 asked 142 potential coachees. Coaches believed SFC fit task-level goals better, but for coachees the type of goal barely mattered — what predicted preference was cognitive style: action-oriented people favored solution-focused coaching, while reflective, evaluative people favored cognitive-behavioral coaching. Expected effectiveness of the two approaches was comparable overall.

**Why it matters:** Organizations buying coaching often specify a methodology for a goal; this suggests the coachee's thinking style is the more consistent basis for a good match, and that coaches' own assumptions about goal-method fit may not reflect what clients want.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** HR and L&D buyers should question vendor claims that a particular coaching method suits a particular type of development goal, and instead consider screening employees on cognitive style — or hiring coaches who can flex between approaches.
- **For general readers:** If you've had coaching that didn't land, the method may simply have mismatched how you think: doers tend to want forward-looking, solution-first sessions; reflectors tend to want to unpack the thinking behind the behavior.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical vignettes rating anticipated attractiveness and effectiveness, not actual coaching outcomes; modest samples (N=198 and N=142); no measurement of whether preferred approaches actually work better.

### 18. When getting what you were promised backfires: the repatriate paradox

**Journal:** *Career Development International* | **Published:** 2026-09-12
**Work ID:** W7212391861 | **DOI:** 10.1108/cdi-08-2025-0451

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 117 German employees on international assignments and again one year after their return home, measuring the gap between what they expected post-return and what they actually got. Fulfilled expectations about career advancement raised satisfaction with repatriation and indirectly lowered turnover intentions, especially for non-managers. But fulfilled expectations about transferring the knowledge they had gained abroad were linked to *lower* satisfaction and *higher* intention to quit, at every hierarchical level.

**Why it matters:** Companies spend heavily on international assignments and then lose returnees; this suggests the loss is not simply about broken promises, but about asking repatriates to hand over hard-won expertise without recognition or reward.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you manage returning expatriates, audit what you are actually asking them to do post-return: being used as a knowledge conduit may feel like an unpaid extraction rather than a career step. Pair any knowledge-transfer role with visible promotion, pay or status, and treat career-advancement promises to non-managerial returnees as the highest-risk ones to break.
- **For general readers:** For anyone taking an overseas posting: the finding hints that the valuable thing you bring home can quietly become a burden, and that negotiating what happens after you return matters as much as negotiating the assignment itself.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=117) from a single country and nationality group; self-report on both expectations and outcomes; met expectations measured as difference scores, which are statistically noisy; turnover intention, not actual turnover; correlational despite the time lag.

### 19. Followers rated a female leader's vision as more competent than the same vision from a man

**Journal:** *Journal of Organizational Behavior* | **Published:** 2026-09-14
**Work ID:** W7212844454 | **DOI:** 10.1002/job.70129

**The study:** Combining an experimental vignette study with a three-wave leader-follower field study, researchers tested whether visionary leadership — an assertive, agentic behavior — plays differently depending on leader and follower gender. Women who led with vision were perceived as more competent than men doing the same, consistent with expectancy violation rather than backlash; those competence perceptions predicted follower identification with the leader and, in turn, support for organizational change. The pattern was strongest among male followers.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the common assumption that women pay a penalty for assertive leadership, and points to vision-setting as one agentic behavior where female leaders may get an advantage rather than a backlash.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers rolling out change should look at who is delivering the vision: this suggests female leaders articulating a compelling future may win more buy-in, particularly from skeptical male staff. Worth testing locally before treating it as a rule — the effect depends on the behavior being a clear, role-appropriate vision rather than directive or dominant assertiveness.
- **For general readers:** The study is a window into how stereotypes bend rather than only punish: when someone exceeds the low expectations people hold for their group, the reaction can be unusually positive — a dynamic familiar to anyone who has been underestimated at work.

**Caveats:** The vignette portion uses hypothetical leaders rather than real work decisions; abstract does not report sample sizes or the field study's country and occupation; competence and identification measures are follower self-report; mechanism inferred rather than directly manipulated in the field.

### 20. Years out of the workforce raising children built skills employers overlook

**Journal:** *Journal of Career Development* | **Published:** 2026-09-11
**Work ID:** W7212253583 | **DOI:** 10.1177/08948453261486762

**The study:** Researchers conducted in-depth interviews with nine South Korean women who returned to paid employment after extended periods in which childcare and household management were their main work. Open coding surfaced seven skill categories the women said they developed: communication, perspective-taking, emotion regulation, relational care, flexibility and adaptability, task planning and coordination, and reflective and contextual awareness.

**Why it matters:** Career gaps for caregiving are routinely read as skill decay; this gives returners and the counselors who advise them a concrete vocabulary for what was actually learned during the gap.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Hiring managers screening out résumé gaps may be discarding candidates with well-developed coordination and emotion-regulation skills; recruiters could test structured interview questions that probe caregiving-period competencies rather than treating the gap as missing data.
- **For general readers:** For anyone preparing to re-enter the workforce after time at home, the research offers language for the interview question nobody can answer well: what did you do during those years?

**Caveats:** Very small qualitative sample (N=9), single country with distinctive gender and labor-market norms; skills are self-perceived, with no independent assessment or link to actual job performance; not generalizable in a statistical sense.

### 21. The proactive career playbook works — but it comes with more anxiety, not less

**Journal:** *Journal of Career Development* | **Published:** 2026-09-10
**Work ID:** W7204500576 | **DOI:** 10.1177/08948453261486054

**The study:** An online survey of 1,634 students aged 17–29 in emerging adulthood (66% women) tested a chain running from proactive personality and protean career orientation through career adaptability to proactive career behaviors and then to education satisfaction, well-being and perceived employability. The expected serial links held, but the well-being results split: effects on comfort and depression were non-significant, while associations appeared with both enthusiasm and anxiety.

**Why it matters:** The dominant career advice — be adaptable, manage your own career, act proactively — is associated with feeling more employable and more enthusiastic, but not with feeling calmer, which complicates the idea that career self-management is simply good for you.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers running early-career development programs should not assume that pushing proactive career behavior improves well-being across the board; it may raise engagement and activation while leaving anxiety untouched or elevated, which is worth measuring separately in program evaluations.
- **For general readers:** For young people told to take charge of their careers: the data suggest the hustle genuinely helps you feel more employable, but it is not a cure for career anxiety — and that is a normal, documented pattern rather than personal failure.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, single-wave, self-report survey of students, not workers, so causal direction cannot be established and common-method bias is likely; outcomes are education-related rather than job-market outcomes; largely a structural-model paper, with the well-being split as the main novel point.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch converge on the same uncomfortable finding — the symbolic language organizations and leaders use to signal meaning is increasingly decoupled from, and sometimes corrosive to, what they actually do. CEOs' loudest sociopolitical stances are the ones least matched by firm follow-through (W7208750164); supervisors who narrate their own good deeds without crediting others trigger employee disgust rather than elevation (W7167868566); purpose statements only stick when facilitators refuse to resolve executives' discomfort (W7172567426); and unconventional job titles impose real "legibility" and "verification" costs on the people who hold them (W7213226017). Taken together, they describe a credibility economy in which the more visible the gesture, the weaker the substance behind it.

**Why pitch this now:** Companies are in the middle of a public retreat from values statements, DEI language and CEO activism, with many quietly rewriting or deleting commitments they made in the past five years — and employees, already fluent in detecting performance, now have research explaining exactly why the talk reads as hollow.

**Angle:** A feature on the mechanics of corporate sincerity — what the evidence says separates meaning-making that lands from meaning-making that repels — framed for executives, comms leaders and HR teams deciding whether to say anything at all right now, with a secondary read for employees learning to audit their own employers' statements.

**Potential headlines:**
- The Difference Between a Company That Means It and One That's Performing
- Your Values Workshop Failed Because Everyone Left Feeling Good
- How to Read Your CEO's Next Big Statement

**Potential outlets:**
- **Harvard Business Review** — directly addresses the leader-communication decision (how to talk about purpose and social issues without losing credibility) that its executive readership is actively navigating.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — strong fit for the research-driven argument that purpose processes need engineered discomfort rather than consensus facilitation.
- **Fast Company** — covers the branding end of this story, from CEO activism to "chief storyteller" job titles, and would run the signaling-versus-substance framing.
- **The Atlantic** — its work coverage handles the cultural question underneath the data: why workers now instinctively distrust corporate moral language, and what that distrust costs.

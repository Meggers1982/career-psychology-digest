### 1. CEOs quit at the top and at the bottom — it's the middling performers who hang on

**Journal:** *Personnel Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-10-20
**Work ID:** W7223031859 | **DOI:** 10.1111/peps.70047

**The study:** Researchers tested an "achievement goal" account of executive retirement using an archival dataset of 1,840 CEOs of S&P 1500 firms, plus a two-wave survey of 363 executives. They found a U-shaped relationship between firm performance and the likelihood of retiring: executives at high-performing firms leave to lock in a legacy, executives at poorly performing firms leave because they reprioritize away from a goal they can no longer win, and those in the middle stay. Executives' own goal orientations (whether they were chasing mastery or avoiding failure) shifted which mechanism dominated.

**Why it matters:** Boards and succession planners typically read a retirement announcement as a verdict on performance, but the same exit decision can mean "I'm leaving on a high note" or "I'm cutting my losses" — and mid-range performance is the condition most likely to keep an aging leader in place.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** For anyone on a board or in succession planning: build the timing of leadership transitions around performance trajectory, not age, and probe what a departing executive is actually optimizing for. The finding also suggests watching for the stuck middle — competent-but-unremarkable tenures that drift on because neither triumph nor failure forces a decision.
- **For general readers:** The same psychology shows up far below the C-suite: people are most likely to walk away from a job or career when they've either clearly won or clearly lost, and most likely to stay put when results are merely okay. Mediocrity, it turns out, is remarkably sticky.

**Caveats:** The CEO analysis is archival and correlational; firm performance and retirement may both reflect unmeasured forces such as board pressure or health. The survey component is self-report over two waves with a modest executive sample (N=363), and findings come from large U.S. public firms, so they may not generalize to private or smaller companies.

### 2. Jobs that are moderately complex — not simple or hard — make people most likely to want out

**Journal:** *Career Development International* | **Published:** 2026-10-17
**Work ID:** W7222847257 | **DOI:** 10.1108/cdi-06-2026-0475

**The study:** Two studies tested whether job complexity relates to wanting to change occupations in a curved rather than straight-line way. Study 1 analyzed the Second European Skills and Jobs Survey, covering 43,859 employees in 29 European countries; Study 2 used a two-wave survey of 434 Chinese full-time employees. Both found an inverted-U pattern: occupational identity threat — the feeling that your work no longer confirms who you are professionally — peaked at intermediate complexity, and that threat in turn predicted intention to switch careers. Employees with a stronger workplace sense of power felt less threatened overall.

**Why it matters:** Job design is usually discussed in terms of "too easy = boring, too hard = stressful," but this suggests the retention danger zone is the ambiguous middle, where work is complex enough to be demanding but not complex enough to confer a clear professional identity.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers auditing turnover risk should look past the obviously dull and the obviously overloaded roles and examine the in-between jobs — the ones where people can't easily say what expertise they're building. Giving those employees more voice and influence may blunt the identity threat even if the work itself doesn't change.
- **For general readers:** If you're restless at work but can't name why, the problem may not be workload at all — it may be that the job doesn't tell a clear enough story about who you are professionally, which is a different problem with different solutions than burnout.

**Caveats:** Both studies are survey-based and self-report; Study 1 is cross-sectional, and Study 2's two waves cannot firmly establish causation. The outcome is intention to change careers, not actual career change. Complexity is perceived rather than objectively coded.

### 3. The dark side of job crafting: reshaping your role can leave you fighting for your identity

**Journal:** *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-10-14
**Work ID:** W7221798889 | **DOI:** 10.1111/joop.70159

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 24 change managers across six public-sector organizations, treating job crafting as identity work rather than a straightforward well-being booster. They found crafting unfolded as repeating cycles of identity management, driven by ambiguity about the role, conflict with others' expectations, and a sense that one's worth went unrecognized as roles shifted during organizational change. Both approach- and avoidance-oriented crafting produced unintended identity consequences that fed back into the next round, and some people coped by redefining what counted as valuable work — a symbolic form of crafting the authors call alternative value definitions.

**Why it matters:** Job crafting is routinely promoted by employers as a low-cost wellness lever, but this evidence suggests that in unstable roles it can be an emotionally draining act of self-preservation rather than empowerment.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Before telling employees to "craft their jobs," managers in restructuring organizations should ask whether the role itself is legible — crafting advice aimed at people whose mandate keeps shifting may just offload the organization's ambiguity onto individuals. Watch especially for change agents and internal transformation roles, which are built on unstable ground.
- **For general readers:** Many people quietly rewrite their own job to make it bearable. This study describes what that actually feels like from the inside — repeatedly reworking how you define your value when the organization won't do it for you.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interview study with 24 participants in one sector (public-sector change management) and no quantitative outcomes; it illustrates a process rather than establishing how common or consequential it is. Retrospective accounts are vulnerable to recall and self-presentation effects.

### 4. For immigrants, the cultural potluck may be the best career networking event in town

**Journal:** *Journal of Career Assessment* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7223147797 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10690727261496523

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 343 Sudanese immigrants in the Midwestern United States about how they engage with immigrant community-based organizations and what they get out of networking there, using a newly developed measure of CBO engagement. The surprise: participation in the organizations' cultural and social activities — not just their explicitly work-related programming — was significantly related to receiving career sponsorship, work-related assistance, job search help, social support, protection and political guidance, and useful information and ideas.

**Why it matters:** Immigrants face steep barriers to good jobs and often land in underemployment or precarious work; this points to an overlooked, already-existing infrastructure that career counselors, employers and community funders could plug into rather than build from scratch.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Employers trying to recruit or develop immigrant talent, and counselors advising immigrant clients, should treat community organizations as genuine labor-market intermediaries — and notice that the social side of those groups is where sponsorship relationships actually form. Managers can also ask whether their own internal affinity groups are being judged only on their formal programming.
- **For general readers:** The finding is a concrete version of something job seekers hear constantly and rarely believe: the connection that gets you hired is more likely to come from a cultural or social gathering than from a resume workshop.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional, self-report survey with predictors and outcomes from the same respondents, so causal direction is unclear (more employable people may join more activities). The sample is a single national-origin group in one U.S. region, limiting generalizability, and the engagement measure is newly developed.

### 5. Pressure only turns into proactive work when employees have the inner resources to absorb it

**Journal:** *The International Journal of Human Resource Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-22
**Work ID:** W7223306094 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09585192.2026.2750364

**The study:** A three-wave survey followed 318 South Korean frontline service employees over six months, examining how performance pressure (hit your numbers) and citizenship pressure (go above and beyond for colleagues) translate into job crafting and proactive service performance. Performance pressure predicted task crafting, and citizenship pressure predicted relational crafting — but mainly among employees who were high on both mindfulness and self-efficacy at the same time. For those lacking that combination, the pressure did not convert into constructive adaptation.

**Why it matters:** It undercuts the managerial assumption that turning up the pressure reliably produces more initiative: the same demand appears to energize well-resourced employees and simply land on everyone else.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you lead a frontline team, treat pressure as conditional rather than universal fuel — and note that the two kinds of pressure produced different responses, with performance demands pushing task redesign and citizenship demands pushing relationship-building. Investment in confidence and attention-regulation skills may be what determines whether pressure pays off at all.
- **For general readers:** For workers in demanding service jobs, this offers a frame for why the same workload feels productive to one colleague and crushing to another, and why the usual advice to "just be more proactive" lands differently depending on what you have left in the tank.

**Caveats:** All measures are employee self-report, including service performance, raising common-method concerns despite the three-wave design. Single occupation in a single country (South Korean frontline service workers), and three-way interactions are statistically demanding and often fragile in replication.

### 6. Your morning portfolio check may be deciding how bold you are at work

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-24
**Work ID:** W7214167465 | **DOI:** 10.1037/apl0001433

**The study:** Researchers ran a 10-day experience-sampling study with full-time employees in South Korea who personally invest in the stock market, tracking daily morning fluctuations in their holdings alongside workplace behavior, then added two experiments testing the mechanism. On mornings when holdings moved up more than usual, workers reported higher risk propensity and went on to take more risks at work and speak up more; down mornings had the reverse pattern. The experiments showed gains triggered a promotion focus and losses a prevention focus, and the effect was strongest for employees low in long-term orientation.

**Why it matters:** A personal financial event that has nothing to do with the job appears to shape whether employees voice ideas, challenge decisions or take bets at work on any given day — an invisible source of variability in meetings and decision-making.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers scheduling high-stakes decisions, innovation sessions or candid feedback rounds may want to consider that the room's appetite for risk is partly imported from outside; the study suggests leaders should not read a quiet day as disengagement, and should question whether a single meeting's risk-taking reflects genuine judgment or ambient mood.
- **For general readers:** For anyone who checks their investment app before the commute, the finding is a reminder that financial mood bleeds into the workday — and that the morning you feel emboldened to pitch a big idea may have more to do with the market than with the idea.

**Caveats:** The field portion was a single-country sample of self-selected retail investors in South Korea with self-reported behavior over a short 10-day window; the mechanism tests were experiments, not real investment losses. Effects are daily and within-person, so magnitudes are modest.

### 7. How employees explain who got promoted predicts how engaged they are — and women and younger staff tell a different story

**Journal:** *Journal of Vocational Behavior* | **Published:** 2026-10-10
**Work ID:** W7221331977 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jvb.2026.104299

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 3,193 employees at an Italian bank across two waves, splitting the sample to identify and then replicate distinct profiles in how people explain pay rises and promotions. Three stable patterns emerged: meritocratic attributors (it's about competence), institutional attributors (it's about rules and procedures), and relational-structural attributors (it's about networks and gender dynamics). Meritocratic attributors reported the highest engagement, commitment, perceived distributive justice and trust in management; relational-structural attributors the lowest. Older and higher-ranking employees clustered in the merit and institutional profiles; younger employees and women were more often in the relational-structural profile.

**Why it matters:** The causal story employees tell themselves about reward decisions functions as an interpretive layer sitting upstream of fairness judgments — meaning two people watching the same promotion can arrive at opposite conclusions about the organization.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Before overhauling a pay or promotion system, managers could usefully ask what employees believe actually drives advancement; the data suggest the belief that networks and gender matter most is concentrated among women and younger staff, and travels with disengagement and low trust. Transparency about criteria may matter less than visibly disconfirming the network narrative.
- **For general readers:** The study puts numbers on a familiar office conversation — whether someone got the job because they earned it or because they knew someone — and shows that where you sit in the hierarchy shapes which answer you find plausible.

**Caveats:** Single bank in one country, so generalization beyond Italian financial services is untested; outcomes are self-reported alongside attributions, and the design shows associations and profile stability rather than causal direction — it cannot say whether disengagement produces the relational-structural view or the reverse.

### 8. Rewriting your job to help colleagues only pays off in your performance review one way

**Journal:** *Journal of Business and Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-10-15
**Work ID:** W7221245981 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s10869-026-10160-w

**The study:** Researchers introduced and tested "prosocial job crafting" — proactively reshaping task, relational or cognitive boundaries of your role to benefit others. Study 1 developed and validated the scale with 500 US employees; Study 2 matched survey data from 262 Hungarian academic library employees with performance ratings from their eight direct supervisors. Perceived prosocial impact predicted all three forms of crafting, but only task-based prosocial crafting — actually changing what you do — predicted supervisor-rated performance.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the assumption that job crafting is a self-serving activity, while showing that the helping-oriented changes that get recognized are the concrete ones, not shifts in how people frame their work or who they connect with.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers who want helping behavior to show up in performance should look at whether employees are changing actual tasks rather than only building relationships or reframing purpose; employees who quietly take on work that benefits others may be the ones supervisors actually notice.
- **For general readers:** For people who find meaning in helping colleagues, the finding is a mixed message: feeling that your work benefits others reliably drives you to restructure your job, but the relational and mental parts of that effort are largely invisible to the people rating you.

**Caveats:** Study 2 rests on a single occupation in one country (Hungarian academic libraries) with performance ratings from only eight supervisors across 262 employees, raising rater-clustering concerns; the design is cross-sectional, so causal claims about crafting improving performance are not supported. Study 1 relied on an online US employee sample.

### 9. Career skills predict feeling successful later — but only if you act on them

**Journal:** *Journal of Career Assessment* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7222470451 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10690727261498888

**The study:** Italian researchers validated a local version of the Career Competencies Questionnaire with 555 employees, then tested its predictive power in a separate three-wave study of 591 Italian employees. Career competencies — knowing yourself, knowing the market, and networking and self-profiling skills — predicted several facets of subjective career success over time, with "career crafting" (actually taking steps to shape one's career) acting as the behavioral channel through which competencies translated into success.

**Why it matters:** It supports the practical point that self-knowledge and labor market knowledge don't produce career satisfaction on their own; the payoff runs through concrete actions people take to reshape their work and trajectory.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Organizations running career development programs may be measuring the wrong thing — the study suggests tracking whether employees convert workshop insight into actual moves (seeking projects, building networks, negotiating role changes) rather than whether their self-assessment scores improved.
- **For general readers:** For individuals, the finding reframes career advice: reflection and market research are the starting point, not the deliverable, and the people who report feeling successful are the ones who crafted something out of what they learned.

**Caveats:** Self-report throughout and all outcomes are subjective career success rather than objective moves like promotions or pay; Italian employee samples only, and a substantial share of the paper is scale validation rather than new substantive discovery. Three waves establish temporal ordering but not causation.

### 10. Why people who want a new job inside their own company never make the move

**Journal:** *Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7223284391 | **DOI:** 10.1111/apps.70148

**The study:** Researchers followed 26 employees for a year, all of whom said at the outset that they wanted to move into a different role within their current organization, and used qualitative comparative analysis to identify which combinations of personal and organizational conditions led to "career inaction" — wanting the move but never making it. Four distinct pathways to inaction emerged, combining individual decision-making factors with contextual ones, rather than organizational barriers alone. The work tests career inaction theory, which argues that unrealized career desires are a distinct phenomenon from realized transitions and deserve study in their own right.

**Why it matters:** Internal mobility is how companies build skills and retain people, yet most internal talent-management thinking focuses on opening up roles rather than on why employees who want them stall. Understanding the individual-level decision traps suggests managers can intervene with conversations and nudges, not just job postings.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers could audit the gap between stated career desires in development conversations and what actually happened 12 months later — this study suggests the blockage often sits in how employees make (or avoid) the decision, not only in whether an opening existed. Worth testing whether deadlines, sponsorship or structured check-ins convert intent into action.
- **For general readers:** Most people have at some point wanted a different job inside their own employer and simply let the year go by. This research gives a name to that drift — career inaction — and suggests it follows recognizable patterns rather than being personal weakness.

**Caveats:** Very small sample (N=26) in a qualitative design; QCA identifies configurations, not causal effects, and findings do not generalize statistically. Single-year horizon and limited information on sector and country.

### 11. Expats who adapt well do better abroad — and the bigger the culture gap, the more it pays off

**Journal:** *The International Journal of Human Resource Management* | **Published:** 2026-09-22
**Work ID:** W7213985838 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09585192.2026.2736063

**The study:** A survey of 325 expatriates tested whether career adaptability — a self-regulatory capacity for coping with career change — was linked to three indicators of a sustainable career: health, happiness and productivity. Career adaptability was positively associated with all three. Cultural distance between home and host country moderated the happiness link: the larger the cultural gap, the stronger the relationship between adaptability and happiness, and the direction of the gap (host values higher or lower than home) also appeared to matter.

**Why it matters:** Companies sending staff on international assignments typically screen for technical skill and past performance; this suggests adaptability is most consequential precisely in the hardest postings, where cultural distance is greatest.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you manage global mobility, consider matching assignment difficulty to adaptability rather than treating all postings as equivalent — and ask whether pre-departure support builds adaptive capacity or just delivers country briefings. The evidence is correlational, so treat it as a hypothesis to test in your own assignee data.
- **For general readers:** For anyone weighing a move abroad, the finding hints that the distant, unfamiliar posting may be where a flexible, exploratory mindset counts most for whether you end up happy there.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional self-report survey with predictor and outcomes from the same source — no causal inference and likely common-method inflation. Expatriate samples are heterogeneous and the nationalities, industries and assignment types are not clear from the abstract; the moderation effect is reported for happiness only.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch converge on the limits of the "own your career, craft your own job" model that employers have adopted as a low-cost development strategy. Job crafting turns out to be exhausting identity work when roles are unstable (**The dark side of job crafting**, W7221798889), pressure only converts into proactive crafting among employees who already have mindfulness and self-efficacy to spare (**Pressure only turns into proactive work**, W7223306094), only concrete task changes register with supervisors while relational and cognitive effort stays invisible (**Rewriting your job to help colleagues**, W7221245981), career competencies pay off only when converted into action (**Career skills predict feeling successful later**, W7222470451), and employees who want an internal move routinely let a year pass without making it (**Why people who want a new job inside their own company never make the move**, W7223284391). Taken together: the self-managed career works best for people who are already well-resourced and whose roles are already legible.

**Why pitch this now:** Flattened hierarchies, shrinking L&D budgets and the rise of internal talent marketplaces have pushed career development from the employer's ledger to the employee's to-do list, just as restructuring makes roles less stable — precisely the condition in which this research says self-directed crafting backfires.

**Angle:** A reported feature on what employers quietly handed to workers when they stopped managing careers, using the evidence to separate who can actually use "craft your job" advice from who is just absorbing the organization's ambiguity — framed for managers in business press, and for mid-career readers in general-interest outlets as a reality check on self-improvement career advice.

**Potential headlines:**
- What Happens When Companies Hand Career Development Back to Employees
- Job Crafting Was Sold as Free Wellness. For Unstable Roles, It's a Second Job.
- The Year You Spend Wanting a Different Job and Not Applying For It

**Potential outlets:**
- *Harvard Business Review* — has published heavily on job crafting and internal talent marketplaces, and this is the evidence-based correction to its own earlier framing.
- *Fast Company* — covers the shift to self-directed careers and internal mobility platforms as a workplace-design story, not just an HR one.
- *The Atlantic* — fits its ongoing interest in how work ideology reassigns institutional problems to individual willpower.
- *Refinery29* (Work & Money) — the inaction and invisible-effort findings map directly onto its readership of younger workers who are told to advocate for themselves and can't see why it isn't working.

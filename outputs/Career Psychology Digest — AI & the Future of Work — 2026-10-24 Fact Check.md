### Study 2: Workers managed by algorithms start treating customers as less than human
**Work ID:** W7218741843 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline for correlational survey** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Workers managed by algorithms start treating customers as less than human"
- **Abstract says:** "Our findings suggest that algorithmic management is positively associated with workers' dehumanization of customers, whereas their dehumanization of customers is negatively associated with customer-oriented behavior."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports associations from a three-wave self-report survey; the headline states a causal process ("start treating"), which the design cannot establish even though the caveat notes the limitation.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to an associational claim, e.g. "Workers who feel managed by algorithms are more likely to see customers as less than human"."

### Study 4: Actors, directors and screenwriters are improvising three very different responses to AI
**Work ID:** W7220851400 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unsupported quantifier** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Many participants reported identity ambiguity, with blurred lines between human originality, technological assistance and professional legitimacy."
- **Abstract says:** "More critically, AI also generates identity ambiguity and fragmentation, blurring boundaries between human originality, technological assistance, and professional legitimacy."
- **Problem:** The abstract presents identity ambiguity as a thematic finding, not as something counted across "many participants"; the proportion is unverifiable from the abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "The analysis also surfaced identity ambiguity, with blurred lines between..."."

### Study 5: Gig workers go the extra mile when the algorithm seems fair — but privacy monitoring backfires
**Work ID:** W7204647820 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Reinterpretation reverses the measured variable** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It indicates that how workers interpret an algorithm matters as much as what the algorithm does, and that perceived data intrusion is a distinct problem not fixed by making the system fairer."
- **Abstract says:** "Autonomy legitimacy and fairness legitimacy were positively related to proactive service behavior, whereas privacy legitimacy was negatively related."
- **Problem:** Privacy legitimacy is workers' judgment that the system's handling of privacy is legitimate; a negative relationship means higher privacy legitimacy went with less proactive service, not that perceived intrusion reduces it. The headline ("privacy monitoring backfires") and the manager angle ("improving fairness will not offset the cost of feeling surveilled") invert the direction of the measured construct.
- **Suggested fix:** "State the finding as the abstract does — judging the algorithm legitimate on privacy grounds was associated with *less* proactive service, an unexplained counterintuitive result — and drop the "surveillance backfires" framing."

**Issue B. Mediation simplified** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Autonomy and fairness legitimacy predicted more proactive service, operating through autonomous motivation"
- **Abstract says:** "Autonomous and controlled motivation served as distinct mediating mechanisms."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports two distinct mediating pathways; the digest mentions only autonomous motivation.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that both autonomous and controlled motivation acted as distinct mediators, with peer support moderating only the autonomous pathway."

### Study 6: Fifty years of Canadian census data: immigrants still earn less — and are concentrated in the wrong corners of the digital economy
**Work ID:** W7222280836 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Positioning result applies to two cohorts only** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "immigrants are less likely than comparable native-born workers to hold full-time jobs in higher-earning industries"
- **Abstract says:** "For two recent cohorts, an earnings-based positioning measure combines industry mean annual earnings with full-time or part-time status... Across the two positioning cohorts, immigrants are less likely than comparable native-born workers to hold full-time jobs in higher-earnings industries"
- **Problem:** The digest presents a result limited to two recent arrival cohorts as if it applied across all ten cohorts analyzed.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add "for the two most recent arrival cohorts analysed"."

**Issue B. "Never arrives" overstates incomplete convergence** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Across five decades of Canadian data, for many cohorts the answer is that full convergence never arrives."
- **Abstract says:** "Entry earnings gaps and incomplete cohort convergence persist, especially among women and recent cohorts."
- **Problem:** "Incomplete convergence" within the observation window is not the same as convergence never occurring; the abstract makes no claim about the eventual endpoint.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "for many cohorts, convergence remains incomplete across the period observed"."

### Study 7: Letting AI make the final call on HR decisions damages a company's reputation
**Work ID:** W7204855159 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Design label not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Experimental studies with external observers judging described scenarios rather than real hiring or firing decisions"
- **Abstract says:** "Across three studies, we show that when AI rather than human HR specialists holds final decision authority over consequential HR decisions, external stakeholders infer greater employee objectification, which, in turn, damages organizational reputation."
- **Problem:** The abstract says only "three studies"; whether they were experiments with scenario vignettes is unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "three studies whose designs and samples are not specified in the abstract" rather than asserting scenario experiments."

### Study 8: Workers who see AI coming are less likely to do the career work that would protect them
**Work ID:** W7213541995 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Moderation presented as a main effect** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Workers who see AI coming are less likely to do the career work that would protect them"
- **Abstract says:** "STARA awareness weakens the positive relationship between future work self and career crafting, thereby reducing the indirect effect of future work self on perceived employability."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only that STARA awareness attenuates the future-work-self→career-crafting path; it does not report that higher STARA awareness lowers career crafting overall. The headline, and the line "The people most alert to AI disruption appear to be the ones whose career initiative stalls," assert a direct negative effect that is not in the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as conditional, e.g. "When workers see AI coming, a clear picture of their future career stops translating into career-building action"."

### Study 9: People accept AI as a subordinate but not as a boss — and the sticking point is morality, not competence
**Work ID:** W7168435596 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Not competence" overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "driven largely by perceived deficits in fairness, trustworthiness and other moral attributes rather than by doubts about their competence"
- **Abstract says:** "Artificial agents in leader roles face role incongruity, driven largely by perceived deficits in morality (e.g., fairness, trustworthiness)."
- **Problem:** The abstract says morality deficits are the main driver but does not rule competence out — indeed the AARC model retains a task dimension including competence. The digest's exclusive contrast, repeated in the headline and the advice that "demonstrations of accuracy will not resolve it," goes beyond the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say morality-related perceptions are the dominant driver, without asserting that competence plays no role."

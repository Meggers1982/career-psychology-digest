### 1. Software that scores what applicants write — without a black box

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-11-05
**Work ID:** W7225331132 | **DOI:** 10.1037/apl0001438

**The study:** Researchers assembled a large multi-study program (total N=67,652) testing transparent natural language processing "word dictionaries" — simple, published word lists tied to specific constructs — applied to narrative application material such as open-ended responses and essays, across school and employment settings, experimental and operational contexts, and concurrent and predictive designs. Dictionary scores predicted selection ratings and decisions at uncorrected averages in the low .20s and job performance criteria nearer .10, and added incremental prediction beyond cognitive ability and personality tests. They also captured hard-to-measure constructs like motivation without asking candidates to rate themselves, and showed small gender and racioethnic subgroup differences.

**Why it matters:** It offers employers a cheap, auditable alternative to opaque AI hiring algorithms: scoring text candidates have already submitted, with validity evidence and small demographic gaps that can be explained to a regulator or a rejected applicant.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If your organization is being pitched AI resume or video scoring, ask whether the vendor can show you the actual words driving the score. This research suggests explainable dictionary methods already recover a meaningful slice of the signal — and the practical test for a hiring team is whether such scores add anything beyond the assessments they already run.
- **For general readers:** What you write in an open-ended application question is increasingly being analyzed, not just read — and the vocabulary you choose can carry signals about motivation and personality you never consciously disclosed.

**Caveats:** Effect sizes for actual job performance are modest (around .10 uncorrected); evidence is aggregated across heterogeneous studies including school settings and experimental contexts, so operational hiring generalizability varies; dictionary methods tuned on one applicant pool may not transfer to another.

### 2. The quietly powerful hiring test most employers underuse

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-11-02
**Work ID:** W7225104062 | **DOI:** 10.1037/apl0001427

**The study:** A meta-analysis updating 30 years of evidence on job knowledge tests found operational validity for job performance of .34 for job-specific tests versus only .19 for generic, nonjob-specific ones. Validity was higher when tests were given to actual applicants (.32) or current employees (.29) than to people seeking professional certification (.20). Mediation analyses showed job knowledge fully accounted for the link between cognitive ability and performance, partly accounted for the effect of job experience, and did not explain conscientiousness effects at all.

**Why it matters:** It suggests a well-built, job-specific knowledge test can rival or beat general aptitude testing — and clarifies that general mental ability may predict performance largely because smart people acquire job knowledge faster.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you use an off-the-shelf "general knowledge" screen, this is evidence to replace it with one built around the specific work. Also worth noting for internal promotion: knowledge tests performed well with current employees, not just outside applicants.
- **For general readers:** The finding reframes what experience is worth. Experience predicts performance partly because it builds knowledge — meaning what you learned on the job matters more than the years on your resume, and candidates who can demonstrate knowledge may offset a shorter track record.

**Caveats:** Meta-analytic operational validities are corrected estimates, not observed correlations; the evidence is correlational — knowledge tests predict performance rather than proving causation; study quality and criterion measures vary widely across the primary research; some criteria included job knowledge itself, which inflates validity (.41 versus .22).

### 3. A long-recommended fix for racial gaps in hiring probably doesn't work

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-11-05
**Work ID:** W7225329342 | **DOI:** 10.1037/apl0001426

**The study:** Using an updated meta-analytic correlation matrix among common predictors and performance criteria, researchers revisited "criterion weighting" — the classic advice that placing more weight on contextual performance (helping, teamwork, citizenship behavior) rather than task performance would reduce adverse impact in hiring. Contrary to decades of reviews, they found it is typically unlikely to improve diversity and likely to damage criterion validity. Pareto-optimal predictor weighting generally outperformed it, and when validity was held constant, shifting criterion weight toward contextual performance tended to worsen rather than reduce adverse impact. The authors propose a joint optimization approach that tunes predictor and criterion weights together.

**Why it matters:** Organizations trying to narrow hiring gaps may be following textbook advice that backfires — redefining "good performance" toward teamwork and citizenship is not a reliable diversity lever.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If your selection system was redesigned around "culture add" or teamwork criteria partly for diversity reasons, this analysis is a prompt to audit whether it actually moved hiring rates — and to look instead at how predictors are weighted.
- **For general readers:** Debates about fairness in hiring usually focus on what tests employers use. This work shows the definition of "success on the job" that employers quietly choose is just as consequential — and intuitions about which definition is fairer can be wrong.

**Caveats:** This is a modeling and simulation exercise built on meta-analytic corrected correlations, not new hiring data from actual firms; results depend on the assumed correlation matrix and the predictor set; US-centric framing of adverse impact and racial subgroups.

### 4. Making personality tests more "work-related" widened the gap for autistic candidates

**Journal:** *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-11-14
**Work ID:** W7231573809 | **DOI:** 10.1111/joop.70171

**The study:** Researchers had autistic and non-autistic respondents complete both general and work-contextualized personality tests (items reframed to refer to behavior at work) under applicant-like conditions, comparing reactions, mean scores and measurement invariance. Autistic respondents rated personality tests less favorably — seeing them as more invasive and less predictive — though differences were small, and contextualization improved reactions for both groups. But scores rose from general to contextualized versions only for non-autistic respondents, widening score gaps, especially on socially oriented traits, and the contextualized test showed evidence of measurement bias.

**Why it matters:** A design tweak widely promoted to make personality assessment feel fairer and more job-relevant may quietly disadvantage neurodivergent applicants on exactly the traits that drive hiring decisions.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Before adopting work-framed personality items, check subgroup score patterns rather than assuming job-relevant wording is automatically fairer. Better applicant reactions and more equitable outcomes are not the same thing.
- **For general readers:** For autistic job seekers, the shift from "I am talkative" to "At work, I am talkative" is not neutral — it invites comparison against a workplace social script that may not reflect capability.

**Caveats:** Respondents completed tests under applicant-like conditions rather than in a real hiring process, so stakes were simulated; sample size and composition of the autistic subgroup are not reported in the abstract; self-identified autism status; single-study design.

### 5. Transgender applicants were rated less hireable — until the resume said more about them

**Journal:** *The International Journal of Human Resource Management* | **Published:** 2026-11-04
**Work ID:** W7225306551 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09585192.2026.2759042

**The study:** In a between-participants in-basket experiment with Indian professionals, cisgender participants evaluated either a transgender woman or a cisgender woman applying for a nursing role. With no individuating information, the transgender applicant received significantly lower hireability ratings. When the file included counterstereotypical details signaling high warmth and high competence, the gap disappeared entirely — supporting the argument that bias travels through stereotype-based justifications rather than open prejudice.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a concrete mechanism behind hiring discrimination in a country where trans rights are legally recognized but professional exclusion persists, and shows that bias is strongest when evaluators have thin information to go on.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Thin applicant files are where stereotypes do the most work. Structured processes that force evaluators to gather specific evidence of warmth and competence for every candidate — rather than relying on a quick impression — may compress the gaps this experiment found.
- **For general readers:** The study suggests ambiguity is a job seeker's enemy: when an evaluator has little concrete information, assumptions fill the gap. That dynamic affects anyone whose background invites guesswork from a hiring manager.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical in-basket scenario, not real hiring decisions; single occupation (nursing) and single country (India); sample size not stated in the abstract; participants may have moderated responses because social desirability pressures are strong in explicit evaluation tasks.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Counterpoints**
- Last run's "The evidence that hiring tests predict job performance is shakier than the textbooks say" (W7222969019) argued that the field's validity figures rest on concurrent studies of incumbents and assumption-driven range-restriction corrections. This run delivers two studies that sit squarely in that line of fire: "The quietly powerful hiring test most employers underuse" (W7225104062) reports corrected operational validities (.34 job-specific vs. .19 generic), and "A long-recommended fix for racial gaps in hiring probably doesn't work" (W7225329342) is a modeling exercise built entirely on a meta-analytic corrected correlation matrix. Notably, W7225104062 contains its own partial answer to the critique — validity was *higher* among actual applicants (.32) and incumbents (.29) than among certification seekers (.20), which is exactly the applicant-versus-no-stakes comparison W7222969019 says the literature rarely isolates.

**Advances**
- The AI-screening story moves from how candidates feel to what employers could build instead. "When a machine screens you" (W7223109819) found that machine evaluation costs applicants their sense of opportunity to perform while buying the employer an innovation halo; "Software that scores what applicants write — without a black box" (W7225331132) answers the black-box half directly, showing that published word dictionaries recover a meaningful slice of the signal with scores an employer can actually show to a regulator or a rejected applicant.

**Convergent findings**
- Disadvantage keeps surfacing in design details rather than in stated policy. "In Brazil, Black workers report that work meets fewer of their basic needs" (W7224595503) located inequality in autonomy, competence and contribution rather than in headline satisfaction; this run finds the same logic earlier in the funnel — "Making personality tests more 'work-related' widened the gap for autistic candidates" (W7231573809) traces a gap to item wording, and "Transgender applicants were rated less hireable — until the resume said more about them" (W7225306551) traces it to how thin the applicant file is, not to open prejudice.
- The non-Western evidence base keeps widening: India again (W7225306551), following Chile, Germany and India (W7223109819) and Brazil (W7224595503).

**New themes**
- Adverse-impact modeling enters the digest for the first time: W7225329342 overturns decades of advice that weighting contextual performance narrows racial gaps, proposing joint predictor-criterion optimization instead. Nothing in the previous run interrogated the criterion side of the equation.
- Neurodivergence as a selection-psychometrics problem (W7231573809) is new — previous runs treated neurodivergence as an accommodation and inclusion issue (ADHD informal supports, invisible disability), not as a measurement-invariance failure in a test employers buy.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Three of this batch's studies show that the interventions sold as fairness improvements don't deliver, while the thing that actually closed a gap was plain information. Criterion weighting toward teamwork and citizenship "probably doesn't work" and often worsens adverse impact (W7225329342); making personality items work-contextualized improved candidate reactions for everyone but raised scores only for non-autistic respondents and introduced measurement bias (W7231573809); meanwhile, adding counterstereotypical detail to a trans applicant's file erased the hireability gap entirely (W7225306551), and transparent word dictionaries offer auditable scoring where opaque AI does not (W7225331132).

**Why pitch this now:** Employers face bias-audit duties under New York's Local Law 144, the Colorado AI Act and the EU AI Act at the same moment that US political pressure has pushed many of them to quietly retire explicit diversity targets — leaving fairness-by-system-design as the main remaining lever, and this evidence says much of that design folklore is wrong.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the gap between fairness intentions and fairness mechanics in selection systems — which redesigns were adopted because they *felt* more equitable, what the evidence says they did, and why richer applicant information and auditable scoring outperform rewriting the test or redefining good performance. Built for HR and hiring leaders, with a general-interest cut for autistic, trans and other candidates who know firsthand that a "fairer" process can go the other way.

**Potential headlines:**
- Four new studies on what actually narrows hiring gaps — and what only looks like it does
- The fairness fixes that widened the gaps they were built to close
- When your application is thin, somebody's assumptions fill in the rest

**Potential outlets:**
- **Harvard Business Review** — its audience owns the selection-design decisions being overturned here (criterion weighting, contextualized items), and it publishes "the practice you adopted is backfiring" arguments with the modeling evidence attached.
- **Fast Company** — strong on inclusive product and assessment design, and the autistic-candidate measurement-bias finding is a concrete design failure story rather than an abstract DEI one.
- **Wired** — the explainable-dictionary alternative to black-box AI scoring is an algorithmic accountability story its hiring-tech coverage is already set up for.
- **The 19th** — covers gender, work and discrimination policy, and would run the trans hireability experiment and the neurodivergence gap as evidence about how bias operates through process rather than stated animus.

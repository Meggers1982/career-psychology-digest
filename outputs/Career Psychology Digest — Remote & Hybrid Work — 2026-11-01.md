### 1. In scattered virtual teams, being a directive boss is what unlocks shared leadership

**Journal:** *Small Group Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-28
**Work ID:** W7224124147 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10464964261496293

**The study:** Researchers ran a 10-week field study of 392 members across 70 teams at two Fortune 500 companies, testing how formal leaders' directive behavior relates to shared leadership in virtual teams. Directive leadership — setting structure, clarifying roles and establishing support systems — was associated with more shared leadership, working indirectly through team empowerment. The effect was strongest in the least connected teams: those spread across many locations and relying on lean, text-based communication rather than rich media.

**Why it matters:** It contradicts the common assumption that distributed teams need hands-off, empowering managers; in practice, structure appears to be the scaffolding that lets distributed team members start leading each other.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers of hybrid and remote teams can test whether their teams' leadership vacuum stems from too little direction rather than too much: clear roles, explicit norms and decision rules may be what frees members to take initiative. Pay special attention to teams that are geographically scattered and communicating mostly by chat and email, where the structural payoff looked largest.
- **For general readers:** For anyone working remotely who feels invisible or unsure who decides what, the finding reframes a boss who sets explicit structure as an enabler rather than a micromanager — and suggests that ambiguity, not supervision, is what suppresses initiative at a distance.

**Caveats:** Two large US companies only, limiting generalizability; the design is a 10-week field study rather than an experiment, so causal direction between directive leadership, empowerment and shared leadership is not established; key measures are likely team-member self-reports, raising common-method concerns.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Counterpoints**
- The single new study, **"In scattered virtual teams, being a directive boss is what unlocks shared leadership" (W7224124147)**, cuts directly against the dominant thread of the previous digest. Last run's batch pointed toward peer-generated support as the load-bearing structure of distributed work — **"Seven years inside a company that went remote: psychological safety got stronger, not weaker" (W7203941475)**, where safety was built bottom-up by colleagues rather than leaders, and **"Return-to-office works better when teammates, not bosses, provide the support" (W7204438985)**, where teammate compassion outlasted supervisor dependability. The new field study says the formal leader is not incidental after all: directive behavior, routed through team empowerment, is what makes lateral leadership emerge in the first place, and it mattered *most* in the most dispersed, text-mediated teams.
- It also complicates the implicit staffing message of **"In global virtual teams, agreeableness was the one trait high performers all shared" (W7169580639)**. That study implied distributed work rewards cooperative, low-assertiveness temperaments; W7224124147 suggests assertive structure-setting at the formal-leader level is precisely what creates room for cooperative contribution below it. Cooperation and direction may be complements rather than substitutes.

**Advances**
- On methodology, W7224124147 partly answers the concern flagged in the last run that the evidence base was leaning qualitative and single-site: 392 members across 70 teams at two Fortune 500 companies, tracked over 10 weeks, is a larger and more quantitative design than the single-firm ethnography (W7203941475) or the 80-worker coworking study (W7202178589). Self-report and non-experimental limits remain, however.

**New themes**
- Communication richness as a moderator is new to this topic: the structural payoff was largest in teams relying on lean, text-based channels. Nothing in the previous digest tested how the *medium* of distributed work changes what leadership has to supply (W7224124147).

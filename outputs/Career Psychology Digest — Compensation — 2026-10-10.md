### 1. A bad boss on one team can make your other boss look like a hero

**Journal:** *Journal of Applied Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-17
**Work ID:** W7213445976 | **DOI:** 10.1037/apl0001429

**The study:** Researchers studied employees who work on several teams at once under different leaders, combining two scenario-based experiments with a field study. They found that when one leader engages in abusive supervision — belittling, hostile behavior — employees report higher satisfaction with a second, less abusive leader on another team, and that satisfaction is in turn linked to stronger performance in the second leader's team. The pattern is a contrast effect: the mistreating boss sets a low bar that makes the alternative leader look better by comparison.

**Why it matters:** As matrixed and multi-team structures spread, leaders' reputations are no longer judged in isolation — a manager may be getting credit (or blame) partly because of what another manager is doing elsewhere in the organization.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** If you manage people who also report to other leaders, your 360 feedback may be measuring the contrast with their other boss as much as your own behavior; the studies suggest leaders should look for unusually glowing or harsh ratings from multi-team employees and check what's happening on the other team. It also means abusive supervision can hide in plain sight — a struggling employee may still perform well for you while being mistreated elsewhere.
- **For general readers:** For anyone who has worked for two managers at once, this helps explain the familiar feeling that one boss is a refuge from the other — and why the comparison, not the person, may be doing some of the work.

**Caveats:** Two of the three studies used hypothetical scenarios rather than real work decisions; the field study design and sample details are limited in the abstract, and satisfaction-to-performance links are correlational.

### 2. Poverty-level pay pushes workers toward the exit — but they work harder while they stay

**Journal:** *Journal of Career Development* | **Published:** 2026-09-07
**Work ID:** W7211910578 | **DOI:** 10.1177/08948453261477682

**The study:** Researchers validated a Japanese version of the Subjective Underemployment Scale among nonregular (non-permanent) employees in Japan across two studies, confirming six dimensions of underemployment: underpay, status, hours, temporary work, field mismatch, and poverty-wage employment. A three-wave study using a random intercept cross-lagged panel model found that within individuals, periods of poverty-wage employment predicted higher turnover intention and lower job satisfaction — and, counterintuitively, higher job performance.

**Why it matters:** It separates underemployment into distinct types and shows that pay-related underemployment, not mismatch of field or hours, is what most damages satisfaction and retention among precarious workers.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers of contract, part-time and other nonregular staff should not read strong performance as a sign of contentment; the data suggest poorly paid workers can be simultaneously productive and actively planning their departure. It argues for diagnosing underemployment by type — pay, hours, status, field — rather than treating it as one lump problem.
- **For general readers:** For workers in insecure jobs, the findings put a name to the different ways a job can be 'beneath you' — and show that low pay, more than working outside your field, is what corrodes satisfaction over time.

**Caveats:** Japanese nonregular employees only, limiting generalizability; self-report measures throughout, including job performance; sample sizes for each study are not given in the abstract; the positive pay–performance finding is within-person and correlational.

### 3. Tying CEO pay to climate goals was linked to more emissions, not fewer

**Journal:** *Academy of Management Perspectives* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215124307 | **DOI:** 10.5465/amp.2025.0113

**The study:** Researchers analyzed changes in scope 1 and scope 2 greenhouse gas emissions at 146 of the world's largest multinational firms from 2012 to 2023, testing which governance actions actually tracked with falling emissions. Common oversight mechanisms — having a climate strategy, board responsibility, internal targets, verification — failed to distinguish firms that cut emissions from firms that increased them. Only externally validated science-based targets (SBTi) were consistently associated with faster reductions, and the longer they had been in place the better; assigning climate responsibility to the CEO and linking CEO compensation to climate goals was associated with higher emissions.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that the incentive-pay fix favored by many boards — bonusing executives on sustainability metrics — is not a reliable signal of real decarbonization, while external third-party validation is.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Boards, compensation committees and anyone evaluating an employer's climate claims should treat internal governance checkboxes and ESG-linked executive bonuses as weak evidence, and look instead for externally validated targets with a multi-year track record. The data also hint at a useful sequence: build internal oversight first, then submit to outside validation.
- **For general readers:** For readers trying to tell genuine corporate climate action from greenwashing, this offers a simple filter — who else has checked the numbers, and for how long.

**Caveats:** Observational analysis of large multinationals only, so associations cannot establish causation; the CEO-compensation finding may reflect reverse causality (high-emitting firms adopting incentive pay) rather than a harmful effect; 146 firms over a single 2012–2023 window.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** All three studies show that the visible indicators organizations rely on to judge people and progress are systematically contaminated by something happening offstage. A manager's glowing ratings may reflect the contrast with an employee's other, abusive boss ("A bad boss on one team can make your other boss look like a hero," W7213445976); strong output from poorly paid nonregular workers coexists with rising turnover intention rather than signaling contentment ("Poverty-level pay pushes workers toward the exit," W7211910578); and linking CEO pay to climate goals tracked with *higher* emissions while only outside validation distinguished real reduction ("Tying CEO pay to climate goals was linked to more emissions," W7215124307). In each case the internal metric -- feedback score, productivity, incentive-linked bonus -- is a weak or inverted read on the thing it is supposed to measure.

**Why pitch this now:** Boards and HR functions are expanding metric-linked pay and continuous-feedback systems at exactly the moment AI-driven performance dashboards and ESG-linked compensation are under regulatory and investor scrutiny, so the question of which numbers can actually be trusted is live.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the measurement problem at the heart of modern pay and performance management -- why the dashboards that look most rigorous are often the easiest to game or misread, and what externally verifiable evidence looks like instead. Aimed at executives, compensation committee members, and HR leaders, with a secondary general-reader version about what your own performance score really measures.

**Potential headlines:**
- Three studies suggest the numbers companies pay bonuses on don't measure what they claim
- Your best performers may be the ones most ready to quit
- The performance score on your review says as much about your other boss as about you

**Potential outlets:**
- *Harvard Business Review* -- its compensation-committee and performance-management readership is precisely the audience being told its favored metrics are unreliable.
- *MIT Sloan Management Review* -- strong track record on governance and measurement critique, and the SBTi-versus-incentive-pay evidence fits its research-translation format.
- *Fast Company* -- would run the counterintuitive "high performers are quietly leaving" framing for a broad management audience.
- *The Atlantic* -- its work coverage suits the bigger cultural argument about what organizations choose to count and what that blinds them to.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **A bad boss on one team can make your other boss look like a hero (Work ID W7213445976):** Why 360 feedback in matrixed organizations may be grading managers on a curve set by their worst colleague -- and how to spot abusive supervision hiding behind one leader's great scores, for a manager-audience column or an HR/performance-management explainer.
- **Poverty-level pay pushes workers toward the exit -- but they work harder while they stay (Work ID W7211910578):** High output from low-paid contract and part-time staff is a retention warning sign, not a success metric; plus the practical idea that underemployment comes in distinct types (pay, hours, status, field) and only the pay kind reliably corrodes satisfaction -- for a workplace news brief or a career-advice column on knowing which kind of "beneath you" job you're in.
- **Tying CEO pay to climate goals was linked to more emissions, not fewer (Work ID W7215124307):** A reader's filter for separating corporate climate action from greenwashing -- externally validated targets with a multi-year record beat internal boards, pledges, and ESG-linked executive bonuses -- for a business/governance explainer or a sustainability news feature, with the reverse-causality caveat stated up front.

### 1. Small business owners badly misjudge what their peers think about climate — and it's holding back action

**Journal:** *Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice* | **Published:** 2026-10-20
**Work ID:** W7223102244 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10422587261485958

**The study:** In a registered report spanning seven countries, researchers surveyed 4,432 owners and leaders of small and medium-sized businesses, asking both what they themselves believed about climate change and sustainability and what they thought their peers believed. A persistent gap emerged: leaders systematically misread the beliefs of other business owners around them. The size of an individual's misperception gap predicted their firm's behavior — those with larger gaps had fewer sustainability policies in place.

**Why it matters:** It suggests a cheap lever for climate action in the SME sector: correcting false beliefs about what other business owners think may move behavior without new regulation or subsidies.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers should question whether "nobody in our industry is doing this" is actually true before using it to justify inaction — and business associations could test simply reporting back what members really believe, as a low-cost nudge.
- **For general readers:** A familiar social trap shows up in the boardroom as much as the schoolyard: people hold back on something they privately support because they wrongly assume everyone else is against it.

**Caveats:** Self-reported beliefs and self-reported sustainability policies from the same respondents; the link between misperception gap and policies is correlational, not causal — the abstract reports no experimental test that correcting beliefs changes behavior. Country-level variation is not detailed in the abstract.

### 2. There is no single recipe for passion at work — and entrepreneurs' version runs on different ingredients than employees'

**Journal:** *Journal of Organizational Behavior* | **Published:** 2026-11-05
**Work ID:** W7225316879 | **DOI:** 10.1002/job.70133

**The study:** Researchers reconceived work and entrepreneurial passion as a configuration of biological, psychological and social conditions rather than a list of separate predictors, and tested the idea using fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis on two time-lagged samples — one of employees, one of entrepreneurs. They identified multiple distinct combinations of conditions that each led to passion, and these pathways differed both between harmonious and obsessive passion and between the two domains. In other words, what fuels an entrepreneur's passion for a venture is largely not what fuels an employee's passion for a job.

**Why it matters:** If passion arises from several equally valid combinations of conditions rather than one universal driver, generic engagement programs that push the same levers for everyone are likely to miss most people.

**Story angles:**
- **For working professionals and managers:** Managers assuming that the conditions that energize founders will also energize employees — autonomy framing, ownership language, mission talk — should check that assumption; the study suggests different groups reach passion by different routes, including routes that produce unhealthy, obsessive passion.
- **For general readers:** For anyone weighing leaving a job to start something, this is a reminder that loving your work and loving your venture are not the same emotion arriving from the same place — and that passion has a compulsive version worth watching for.

**Caveats:** Sample sizes and countries are not reported in the abstract; fsQCA identifies configurations consistent with outcomes rather than causal effects, and time-lagged self-report designs still leave open reverse causation and common-method concerns. The finding is pattern-level and offers little in the way of specific, directly actionable levers.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- Both digests keep landing on the same mechanism: what founders and owners *believe other people think* becomes concrete firm behavior. "Small business owners badly misjudge what their peers think about climate" (W7223102244) shows misread peer beliefs predicting fewer sustainability policies, which rhymes with last run's "North Korean defectors who started businesses built three very different kinds of companies" (W7214760936), where founders' readings of how locals evaluated them shaped mission, hiring and branding, and with "Why women founders post about their worst days" (W7215047180), where anticipated audience judgment drove carefully engineered disclosure. Social evaluation — accurate or not — is doing strategic work in all three.

**Advances**
- The methodological shift flagged last run has arrived. The previous batch was almost entirely qualitative field work and historical case analysis (W7203797024, W7214760936, W7215047180, W7204156044); this batch is a seven-country registered report with 4,432 respondents (W7223102244) and a configurational fsQCA across two time-lagged samples (W7225316879). The questions have moved from thick description of extreme contexts to population-scale measurement and pattern detection.
- Last run's SME-and-region policy story, "The startup policy that grows the economy while hollowing out the towns next door" (W7204156044), argued that aggregate levers miss heterogeneity across places. W7223102244 extends that logic inside the firm: a cheap belief-correction nudge may do work that regulation and subsidy don't, precisely because the binding constraint is perceptual rather than material.

**New themes**
- Climate and sustainability enter the entrepreneurship digest for the first time (W7223102244), and as a behavioral-psychology problem rather than a policy or technology one.
- Passion — and specifically the entrepreneur/employee divergence, plus obsessive versus harmonious forms (W7225316879) — is new. Last run's emotional terrain was endurance and cost under adversity (W7203797024); this one asks what energizes people in ordinary conditions, and finds no single answer.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Both studies in this batch show business leaders acting on a confidently held but wrong model of other people's inner lives. SME owners across seven countries systematically misread what their peers believe about climate, and the bigger the misread, the fewer sustainability policies their firm has (W7223102244); meanwhile, passion turns out to arrive through several distinct configurations that differ between entrepreneurs and employees, meaning the single driver most engagement programs assume simply isn't there (W7225316879). The shared implication is that the costly error isn't a lack of motivation or conviction — it's bad mind-reading, and the fix is measurement rather than exhortation.

**Why pitch this now:** Firms are being pushed to justify both sustainability spending and employee-engagement budgets under cost pressure, and both decisions are routinely made on a gut read of "what people around here actually want" — a read this research says is unreliable at scale.

**Angle:** A reported piece on the mind-reading problem in business — how leaders consistently misjudge peers and staff, what that misjudgment costs in climate inaction and wasted engagement programs, and the cheap diagnostic alternative of just asking and reporting back; written for managers and owners, with enough social psychology to work for a general business reader.

**Potential headlines:**
- Business Owners Are Bad at Guessing What Other Business Owners Believe
- Everyone in Your Industry Is Waiting for Someone Else to Go First
- You Are Budgeting Against a Crowd That Doesn't Exist

**Potential outlets:**
- *Harvard Business Review* — pluralistic ignorance with a concrete managerial remedy (survey your members, publish the result) is exactly its evidence-plus-tool format.
- *Business Insider* — strong small-business and sustainability-spending readership, and the "you're wrong about your peers" finding is a clean, shareable news-feature hook.
- *Bloomberg Green* — covers corporate climate decision-making and would run the argument that a perceptual gap, not cost, is gating SME action.
- *Vox* — its behavioral-science-meets-climate coverage is built for a story about a social trap operating in boardrooms.

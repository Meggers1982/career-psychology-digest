### Study 1: A bad boss on one team can make your other boss look like a hero
**Work ID:** W7213445976 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal phrasing for an associational finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A bad boss on one team can make your other boss look like a hero"
- **Abstract says:** "abusive supervision by one leader is associated with more favorable evaluations of an alternative leader... Abusive supervision by one leader is positively related to satisfaction with another leader, which is in turn associated with performance in that leader's team."
- **Problem:** The abstract uses strictly associational language throughout, including for the scenario experiments, and the satisfaction-performance link is correlational. "Can make" reads as an established causal effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to something like "A bad boss on one team is linked to higher satisfaction with your other boss" and keep the associational framing in the body."

**Issue B. Story angle extends beyond tested outcomes** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the studies suggest leaders should look for unusually glowing or harsh ratings from multi-team employees and check what's happening on the other team"
- **Abstract says:** "abusive supervision by one leader is associated with more favorable evaluations of an alternative leader, particularly when the latter leader engages in lower levels of abusive supervision"
- **Problem:** The research tested only more favorable evaluations of the alternative (less abusive) leader; harsh ratings produced by contrast were not examined in the abstract, and no practical rating-audit implication is stated.
- **Suggested fix:** "Limit the angle to unusually favorable ratings from multi-team employees, or note that the reverse (harsher ratings) is an untested extrapolation."

### Study 2: Poverty-level pay pushes workers toward the exit — but they work harder while they stay
**Work ID:** W7211910578 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline for a panel-correlational finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Poverty-level pay pushes workers toward the exit — but they work harder while they stay"
- **Abstract says:** "At the within-person level, poverty wage employment positively predicted turnover intention and job performance and negatively predicted job satisfaction."
- **Problem:** "Pushes" and "work harder" assert causation and behavior change from a three-wave cross-lagged panel of self-reported measures; the digest's own caveat acknowledges the finding is correlational, so the headline contradicts it.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as "Poverty-level pay predicts higher turnover intention — and, oddly, higher reported performance"."

**Issue B. Untested comparison between underemployment types** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "shows that pay-related underemployment, not mismatch of field or hours, is what most damages satisfaction and retention among precarious workers"
- **Abstract says:** "field underemployment positively predicted job satisfaction, turnover intention positively predicted hours discrepancy, and career commitment negatively predicted hours discrepancy"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports no comparison of effect magnitudes across dimensions, and no test of field or hours underemployment predicting turnover intention; hours discrepancy appears as an outcome, not a predictor. The abstract's counterintuitive result that field underemployment positively predicted job satisfaction is also omitted.
- **Suggested fix:** "State only what was tested: poverty-wage employment predicted lower satisfaction and higher turnover intention, while field underemployment unexpectedly predicted higher job satisfaction; drop the "most damages" ranking."

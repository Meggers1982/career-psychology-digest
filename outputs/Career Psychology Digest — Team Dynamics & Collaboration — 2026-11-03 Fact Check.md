### Study 1: Moving to a new job with an old teammate helps you — and hurts your new team
**Work ID:** W7221144841 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Analysis attribution swapped** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the advantage was larger the longer they had played together before; robustness checks showed the individual edge persisted over time"
- **Abstract says:** "in our robustness checks we find that greater shared tenure on the prior team further enhances this advantage. ... Additional analyses show that co-movers' individual performance advantage persists over time in the new team."
- **Problem:** The digest assigns the persistence result to the robustness checks; in the abstract the robustness checks produced the shared-tenure result, while persistence came from additional analyses.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as: "robustness checks showed the advantage was larger the longer they had played together, and additional analyses showed the individual edge persisted over time.""

### Study 2: Teams are stickier than we think — and conflict is the stickiest thing of all
**Work ID:** W7220392932 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Prescriptive leap from cross-lagged correlations** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "engineer early wins deliberately, since the meta-analysis finds performance feeds back into subsequent cohesion, confidence and coordination"
- **Abstract says:** "team performance exhibits consistent feedback loops, predicting subsequent emergent states and processes, which, in turn, predict later performance"
- **Problem:** The meta-analysis synthesizes longitudinal correlations, not interventions; "engineer early wins" treats a predictive feedback loop as a tested managerial lever. The specific states named (cohesion, confidence, coordination) are also the digest's gloss on "emergent states and processes."
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "early performance predicts later team states and processes in this synthesis, so early wins may be worth prioritising — though no intervention was tested.""

### Study 3: A structured kickoff for new research teams led to more grants and more papers
**Work ID:** W7222970844 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline overstates causal chain** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A structured kickoff for new research teams led to more grants and more papers"
- **Abstract says:** "Teams that received the scaffolding intervention showed greater knowledge integration than those in the comparison group, and this increase in integration was associated with stronger downstream performance, including more grants secured, more publications produced, and higher journal impact."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a quasi-experimental effect of the intervention on knowledge integration, and only an association between integration and grants/publications. "Led to more grants and more papers" asserts a causal effect on outputs that the abstract does not claim.
- **Suggested fix:** "Try "A structured kickoff boosted knowledge integration in new research teams — and integration tracked with more grants and papers.""

**Issue B. Unverified timing claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "pays off in hard output months and years later"
- **Abstract says:** "this increase in integration was associated with stronger downstream performance, including more grants secured, more publications produced, and higher journal impact"
- **Problem:** The abstract gives no follow-up window, so "months and years later" is unverifiable from the abstract alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop the time frame or say "in downstream grant and publication outcomes.""

### Study 5: Robot teammates made teams more effective — but only after a few weeks
**Work ID:** W7223169561 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Only after" vs. "particularly in later phases"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Robot teammates made teams more effective — but only after a few weeks"
- **Abstract says:** "Over time, human-robot teams show higher effectiveness than human-only teams, particularly in later project phases."
- **Problem:** The abstract says the advantage was more pronounced later, not that it was absent earlier; "only after a few weeks" asserts a null early effect the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "— and the edge was biggest in the later project phases.""

**Issue B. Interpretation of competence mismatch** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "overselling a robot's capabilities undermines acceptance"
- **Abstract says:** "robot acceptance is shaped ... by the alignment between expected and experienced robot competence"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that alignment matters; it does not specify that the damaging direction is over-promising specifically.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "mismatch between expected and experienced competence undermined acceptance.""

### Study 6: When colleagues get rude, high performers conclude they're being envied — and start playing politics
**Work ID:** W7221801860 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Proactivity standing recast as performance** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "high performers conclude they're being envied"
- **Abstract says:** "this relationship is strengthened by employee proactivity standing ... particularly when employee proactivity standing was high"
- **Problem:** The moderator is how proactive an employee is seen to be, not job performance; the headline (and the "standout performer" framing in the angles) would lead readers to apply the finding to high performers generally.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "notably proactive employees" in the headline and angles instead of "high performers"/"standout performer.""

**Issue B. Mild causal verb for multi-wave survey** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "experiencing incivility from coworkers predicted a stronger sense of being envied"
- **Abstract says:** "experienced incivility was positively associated with the perception of being envied"
- **Problem:** The abstract uses associational language throughout; "predicted" nudges toward causation, though the digest's caveats do note the correlational design.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "was associated with" in the body text."
